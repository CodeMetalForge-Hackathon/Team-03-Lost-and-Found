'''Faneuil Hall''' ({{IPAc-en|ˈ|f|æ|n|əl}}, {{IPAc-en|ˈ|f|ʌ|n|əl}}, or {{IPAc-en|ˈ|f|æ|n|j|əl}}) is a historic building in [[Downtown Boston]], Massachusetts, United States. Opened in 1742, the building was designed by artist [[John Smibert]] as a marketplace and meeting hall. Faneuil Hall is sometimes referred to as "the Cradle of Liberty", having been the site of many speeches, debates, and other events over its history. Over the years, the building, and especially its Great Hall meeting room, has received commentary for its symbolism. Faneuil Hall is part of [[Boston National Historical Park]] and is designated as a [[National Historic Landmark]]. It is owned by the Boston government and operated as part of the [[Faneuil Hall Marketplace]].

Faneuil Hall hosted regular events and speeches before the [[American Revolutionary War]], along with a marketplace at ground level. The original building burned down in 1761 and was rebuilt over the next decade. Faneuil Hall was remodeled and expanded by [[Charles Bulfinch]] in 1806, and it was renovated again in 1827 after the nearby [[Quincy Market]] opened. The interiors were rebuilt of noncombustible materials in 1898–1899, and [[Cram and Ferguson]] oversaw another renovation between 1923 and 1925. Further modifications took place throughout the 20th century, including several cleaning, fireproofing, restoration, and accessibility projects. The building was renovated again in the 21st century.

Faneuil Hall has four stories including the attic, and is made of red brick, divided vertically into several [[Bay (architecture)|bays]]. There are entrances and arched [[sash windows]] on the first floor, and additional windows on the other stories, each separated by [[pilaster]]s of varying designs. The slate [[gable roof]] has a [[cupola]] and a grasshopper-shaped weathervane. Inside, the basement is used for offices and education, while the first floor contains a market area measuring {{Convert|76|by|100|ft}}. A stair from the main eastern entrance ascends through the building. On the second and third floors is the Great Hall, measuring {{Convert|28|ft}} high and {{Convert|76|by|76|ft}} across with various pieces of artwork. There are also small offices on the second floor. The fourth-story attic houses the [[Ancient and Honorable Artillery Company of Massachusetts]], and there is a commandery room just beneath the cupola.

== History ==
A public marketplace in the town (later city) of [[Boston]] was first organized in 1658, during the [[American colonial period]], at the [[Old State House (Boston)|Old State House]]'s future site.<ref name="Amadon p. B4" /><ref name="Quincy pp. 6–7">{{Harvnb|Quincy|2003|ps=.|pages=6–7}}</ref> The market was effectively banned in 1695,<ref name="Quincy p. 7">{{Harvnb|Quincy|2003|ps=.|page=7}}</ref> and several attempts to establish a public market over the next several decades were unsuccessful.<ref name="BLC pp. 9–10">{{Harvnb|Boston Landmarks Commission|1994|ps=.|pages=9–10}}</ref> Northern, central, and southern markets were established in 1734,<ref name="Amadon p. B4" /><ref name="BLC pp. 9–10" /> but the central market was demolished by rioters three years later.<ref name="Amadon p. B4" /><ref name="NPS p. 3" /><ref name="Quincy p. 12">{{Harvnb|Quincy|2003|ps=.|page=12}}</ref>

=== 18th century ===
==== First Faneuil Hall ====
In 1740, colonial merchant and slave trader [[Peter Faneuil]] offered to donate a building "for a market for the sole use, benefit, and advantage of the town", provided the [[Boston Board of Selectmen]] voted in favor.<ref name="Brown p. 80">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|page=80}}</ref><ref name="BLC p. 9">{{Harvnb|Boston Landmarks Commission|1994|ps=.|page=9}}</ref> Faneuil's offer also required that the market be regulated and continually used as such.<ref name="BLC p. 10">{{Harvnb|Boston Landmarks Commission|1994|ps=.|page=10}}</ref> The town voted 367–360 to accept the offer on July 14, 1740;<ref name="BLC p. 10" /><ref name="Brown p. 82">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|page=82}}</ref><ref name="Pula p. 45">{{cite book |last=Pula |first=James S. |url=https://www.archive.org/details/frenchinamerica100pula/page/45 |title=The French in America, 1488–1974: A Chronology & Factbook |date=1975 |publisher=Oceana Publications |isbn=978-0-379-00515-8 |page=45|url-access=registration}}</ref> the vote was controversial, and citizens challenged its validity.<ref name="BLC p. 10" /><ref name="Quincy p. 14">{{Harvnb|Quincy|2003|ps=.|page=14}}</ref> The selectmen identified a {{Convert|100|by|40|ft|adj=on}} site in [[Merchants Row (Boston)|Merchants Row]], which had been occupied by the 1734 central market,<ref name="BLC p. 10" /><ref name="Brown p. 82" /> and approved its acquisition at a meeting on September 2, 1740.<ref name="Quincy p. 14" /><ref name="BLC pp. 10–11">{{Harvnb|Boston Landmarks Commission|1994|ps=.|pages=10–11}}</ref><ref name="Brown p. 83">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|page=83}}</ref> Funded in part by profits from slave trading,<ref>{{cite web |url=http://historyofmassachusetts.org/faneuil-hall-was-built-with-slave-money/ |title=Was Faneuil Hall Built with Slave Money? |date=March 13, 2012 |access-date=May 30, 2018}}</ref><ref name="p576009702">{{Cite news |date=March 26, 1899 |title=A New Faneuil Hall: The Cradle of Liberty is Being Rebuilt |work=San Francisco Chronicle |page=9 |id={{pq|576009702}}}}</ref> the building was designed by artist [[John Smibert]] in the style of an English country market.<ref name="dailyfreepress.com">{{cite web |url=http://dailyfreepress.com/2010/10/05/unearthing-bostons-past/ |title=Unearthing Boston's Past – The Daily Free Press |website=dailyfreepress.com |date=October 5, 2010 |access-date=May 30, 2018 |archive-date=October 16, 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131016054929/http://dailyfreepress.com/2010/10/05/unearthing-bostons-past/ |url-status=live }}</ref><ref name="NPS p. 3">{{harvnb|National Park Service|1966|ps=.|page=3}}</ref><ref name="Brown p. 87">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|page=87}}</ref> It was built primarily by mason Joshua Blanchard and carpenter Samuel Ruggles;<ref name="NPS p. 3" /><ref name="Brown p. 87" /> a cornerstone with Blanchard's initials was placed outside the original building.<ref name="n190143845">{{Cite news |date=December 15, 1895|title=Who Built Faneuil Hall?|via=Newspapers.com|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-boston-globe-who-built-faneuil-hall/190143845/|access-date=January 30, 2026|work=Boston Daily Globe |issn=0743-1791|pages=17}}</ref> Faneuil Hall was originally envisioned as a single-story market house before a second story for the town government was added to the plans.<ref name="p514004301">{{cite news
|id={{ProQuest|514004301}}|title=Faneuil Hall, 200 Years Old, Shines in American History: He Knew His Way Around Enlarged in 1805|date=August 24, 1942|page=2|work=The Christian Science Monitor|issn=0882-7729}}</ref><ref name="Amadon p. B6">{{Harvnb|Amadon|1970|ps=.|page=B6}}</ref>

The building was substantially completed in August 1742<ref name="NPS p. 3" /><ref name="Brown p. 84">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|page=84}}</ref> and opened on September 10 of that year.<ref name="Brown p. 84" /><ref name="BLC p. 11">{{Harvnb|Boston Landmarks Commission|1994|ps=.|page=11}}</ref><ref name="Detwiller p. 7">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=7}}</ref> After some debate, the building was named Faneuil Hall for its donor,<ref name="Brown pp. 85–86">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|pages=85–86}}</ref> and the selectmen began hosting meetings there on October 13.<ref name="NPS p. 3" /><ref name="BLC p. 11" /><ref name="Brown p. 86">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|page=86}}</ref> The original Faneuil Hall bore similarities to 17th-century English marketplaces.<ref name="Detwiller p. 7" /> The two-story building, clad in brick,<ref name="Pula p. 45" /><ref name="BLC p. 11" /><ref name="Brown p. 86" /> was designed in the early [[Georgian architecture|Georgian]] style.<ref name="NPS p. 3" /><ref name="Detwiller p. 7" /> It had an [[Arcade (architecture)|arcaded]] market and offices at ground level; an assembly hall and selectmen's chamber on the second story; and an [[armory]] and [[Assessor (law)|assessor]]'s office in the attic.<ref name="BLC p. 11" /><ref name="Quincy pp. 17–18">{{Harvnb|Quincy|2003|ps=.|pages=17–18}}</ref> The assembly hall could fit 1,000 people.<ref name="Amadon p. B6" /><ref name="Quincy pp. 17–18" /> In addition to the selectmen's chamber, the town government had offices in at least three additional rooms.<ref name="BLC p. 11" /><ref name="Detwiller p. 9">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=9}}</ref> After Faneuil's death in 1743, the town displayed his [[coat of arms]] at Faneuil Hall in his honor.<ref name="Detwiller p. 8">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=8}}</ref><ref name="Stark j577">{{cite book |last=Stark |first=James Henry |url=https://archive.org/details/loyalistsofmassa00star/ |title=The Loyalists of Massachusetts and the Other Side of the American Revolution |publisher=J.H. Stark |year=1910 |page=231 |access-date=July 4, 2026|location=Boston|language=en}}</ref>

The marketplace struggled to attract customers or merchants,<ref name="BLC p. 12" /><ref name="Amadon p. B72">{{Harvnb|Amadon|1970|ps=.|page=B7}}</ref> and the very idea of a marketplace remained controversial.<ref name="Quincy p. 20">{{Harvnb|Quincy|2003|ps=.|page=20}}</ref> The first merchant leased a stall there in December 1742,<ref name="Detwiller p. 8" /><ref name="BLC pp. 11–12" /> but the next merchant did not sign a lease for about a year.<ref name="BLC pp. 11–12">{{Harvnb|Boston Landmarks Commission|1994|ps=.|pages=11–12}}</ref><ref name="Brown p. 92">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|page=92}}</ref> Most activity took place in the Great Hall, which was used for town meetings,<ref name="Amadon p. B72" /><ref name="p514004301" /> public celebrations, and military drills.<ref name="BLC p. 12">{{Harvnb|Boston Landmarks Commission|1994|ps=.|page=12}}</ref> Social events also took place there,<ref name="NYT b221">{{cite web |date=June 8, 1901 |title=Faneuil Hall; Its History and the Romance of the Family.* |url=https://www.nytimes.com/1901/06/08/archives/faneuil-hall-its-history-and-the-romance-of-the-family.html |access-date=January 24, 2026 |website=The New York Times |issn=0362-4331}}</ref> and some of Boston's early slave auctions took place near Faneuil Hall.<ref name="dailyfreepress.com" /> The [[Ancient and Honorable Artillery Company of Massachusetts]] (colloquially the Ancients) moved into the building's attic in 1746,<ref name="BLC p. 12" /><ref name="The National Magazine y8882">{{cite book |url=https://www.google.com/books/edition/The_National_Magazine/wDu1KmcrZHIC?hl=en&gbpv=1&pg=PA259&printsec=frontcover |title=The National Magazine |date=1930 |publisher=Bostonian Publishing Company |page=259 |access-date=January 24, 2026}}</ref> and the [[Massachusetts Governor's Council]] briefly moved to Faneuil Hall the next year after State House burned down.<ref name="BLC p. 12" /><ref name="Detwiller p. 10">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=10}}</ref> The Governor's Council moved out in 1748 once repairs to the State House were completed.<ref name="Detwiller p. 10" /> Due to low patronage, the ground-floor market experienced several extended closures in 1747–1748, 1752–1753, and 1759.<ref name="BLC p. 12" /><ref name="Quincy p. 20" /> The building survived an earthquake in 1753,<ref name="n190068991">{{Cite news |date=January 8, 1899 |title=Curing Faneuil Hall |via=Newspapers.com|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-boston-globe-curing-faneuil-hall/190068991/ |access-date=January 29, 2026 |work=The Boston Globe |issn=0743-1791 |pages=17}}</ref> though the weathervane was damaged.<ref name="Detwiller p. 15" /><ref name="Brown p. 137" /> The first floor had a notary public and a naval office by 1761.<ref name="Detwiller p. 9" />

==== Reconstruction and pre-Revolutionary activity ====
On January 13, 1761, the first Faneuil Hall was destroyed by fire.<ref name="NPS p. 3" /><ref name="Detwiller p. 12">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=12}}</ref><ref name="Brown p. 96">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|page=96}}</ref> With nothing but the brick walls remaining,<ref name="acab">{{Appletons'|wstitle=Faneuil, Peter|year=1900|inline=1}}</ref> the selectmen temporarily met in any building that could accommodate them.<ref name="Detwiller p. 12" /><ref name="Brown p. 96" /> The selectmen voted to repair Faneuil Hall that March,<ref name="Brown p. 96" /><ref name="Detwiller p. 13" /><ref name="Amadon p. B10">{{Harvnb|Amadon|1970|ps=.|page=B10}}</ref> and the [[Massachusetts General Court]], the legislature of the [[Massachusetts Bay Colony]], approved a lottery to raise funds for the building.<ref name="BLC p. 12" /><ref name="Quincy p. 21">{{Harvnb|Quincy|2003|ps=.|page=21}}</ref><ref name="Brown p. 97">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|page=97}}</ref> Onesiphorus Tileston and several other contractors were hired to conduct repairs,<ref name="Brown p. 96" /><ref name="Detwiller p. 15">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=15}}</ref> which were underway by late 1761.<ref name="Detwiller p. 13">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=13}}</ref> The selectmen moved back in during October 1762, as repairs continued.<ref name="Detwiller p. 13" /> The lawyer [[James Otis Jr.]] rededicated Faneuil Hall to the "Cause of Liberty" on March 14, 1763,{{Efn|The 1763 date is given by multiple sources.<ref name="Quincy p. 22" /><ref name="CBS Boston h053">{{cite web |date=July 12, 2013 |title=5 Things You Didn't Know About Faneuil Hall |url=https://www.cbsnews.com/boston/news/5-things-you-didnt-know-about-faneuil-hall/ |access-date=January 26, 2026 |website=CBS Boston |archive-date=August 18, 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250818014454/https://www.cbsnews.com/boston/news/5-things-you-didnt-know-about-faneuil-hall/ |url-status=live }}</ref><ref name="Stark b807" /><ref name="n190194809" /> Some sources give a different rededication date of March 14, 1764.<ref name="BLC p. 12" /><ref name="Amadon p. B10" />}} though interior work continued through early 1768.<ref name="Detwiller p. 14">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=14}}</ref> The new Faneuil Hall had the same dimensions as the original structure and could fit 1,000 occupants.<ref name="NPS pp. 3, 5">{{harvnb|National Park Service|1966|ps=.|pages=3, 5}}</ref> It had less woodwork than the original structure<ref name="Quincy p. 21" /><ref name="Amadon p. B10" /> and had a rooftop [[cupola]] rather than a steeple.<ref name="Detwiller p. 15" /> Though the original hall had contained a bell, it was not reproduced, likely due to a lack of money.<ref name="Detwiller p. 17">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=17}}</ref><ref name="Brown p. 133">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|page=133}}</ref>

After its rededication, Faneuil Hall regularly hosted speeches from pro-independence [[Patriot (American Revolution)|Patriots]], such as Otis and [[Samuel Adams]],<ref name="Maloy h872">{{cite web |last=Maloy |first=Mark |date=February 2, 2026 |title=Faneuil Hall |url=https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/faneuil-hall |access-date=February 2, 2026 |website=American Battlefield Trust |archive-date=January 13, 2026 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260113032052/https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/faneuil-hall |url-status=live }}</ref> and was nicknamed the "Cradle of Liberty".<ref name="Stark j577" /><ref name="Brown p. 1242">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|page=124}}</ref> The selectmen allowed both Patriots and pro-British [[Loyalist (American Revolution)|Loyalists]] to host events there, even while banning members of the [[British military]] and local customs officials from using it.<ref name="BLC pp. 12–13">{{Harvnb|Boston Landmarks Commission|1994|ps=.|pages=12–13}}</ref> In the years before the [[American Revolutionary War]], the building was a frequent location for debates and protests against [[Parliament of Great Britain]];<ref name="BLC p. 12" /><ref name="NPS p. 5">{{harvnb|National Park Service|1966|ps=.|page=5}}</ref> for instance, residents protested taxes there in the 1760s.<ref name="NPS p. 5" /><ref name="Quincy p. 23">{{Harvnb|Quincy|2003|ps=.|page=23}}</ref> Because of its large capacity, Faneuil Hall also began hosting sessions of the General Court of Massachusetts in 1764,<ref name="Brown p. 1242" /> and it also hosted meetings of the Massachusetts Convention.<ref name="Amadon p. B11" /><ref name="Quincy p. 26">{{Harvnb|Quincy|2003|ps=.|page=26}}</ref> Other modifications continued throughout the decade; for example, the interior was illuminated in 1767.<ref name="Detwiller p. 15" /> The original weathervane was restored and installed atop the cupola next year.<ref name="Detwiller p. 15" /><ref name="Brown p. 137" /> The market at the ground floor gained popularity as a food market.<ref name="Quincy p. 22">{{Harvnb|Quincy|2003|ps=.|page=22}}</ref>

On October 1, 1768, two thousand British troops of the [[14th Foot]] and [[29th Foot]] landed in Boston to occupy the city and enforce Crown authority. After disputes between the occupational force's commanders and Boston officials over where the troops would be quartered, the 14th Foot was eventually quartered in Faneuil Hall.<ref name="BLC pp. 12–13" /><ref name="Brown p. 127">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|page=127}}</ref><ref name="Quincy p. 23" /> Faneuil Hall continued to host meetings and events in the meantime,<ref name="Brown p. 127" /> and the troops quartered there were ultimately relocated in late 1770.<ref name="Quincy p. 23" /> Events in the 1770s included the [[Boston Tea Party]] protests of 1773,<ref name="NPS p. 5" /><ref name="Snyder i6812">{{cite web|last=Snyder|first=Michael|title=Why Faneuil Hall Is the Perfect Metaphor for the American Revolution's Complicated Definition of Liberty|website=Smithsonian Magazine|date=March 21, 2025|url=https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/faneuil-hall-perfect-metaphor-american-revolution-complicated-definition-liberty-180985633/|access-date=January 24, 2026|archive-date=October 18, 2025|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20251018135657/https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/faneuil-hall-perfect-metaphor-american-revolution-complicated-definition-liberty-180985633/|url-status=live}}</ref> along with regular town meetings and a concert in 1774.<ref name="Brown pp. 130–131">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|pages=130–131}}</ref> Following the [[American Revolutionary War]] breaking out in April 1775, the [[Continental Army]] began a [[siege of Boston]], during which British troops hosted theatrical shows and stored munitions and weapons at Faneuil Hall.<ref name="Brown p. 131">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|page=131}}</ref><ref name="Amadon p. B11">{{Harvnb|Amadon|1970|ps=.|page=B11}}</ref> The marketplace was closed during the occupation.<ref name="n190219459">{{Cite news |last=Harvey|first=Joseph M.|date=July 10, 1949|title=Old Faneuil Hall Market is Doomed|via=Newspapers.com|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-boston-globe-old-faneuil-hall-market/190219459/|access-date=January 31, 2026|work=The Boston Globe |issn=0743-1791|pages=1, [https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-boston-globe-market/190219363/ 26]}}</ref> The British garrison evacuated Boston in March 1776, taking some portraits from the building.<ref name="Quincy p. 28" /><ref name="Amadon p. B11" /><ref name="Brown p. 132">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|page=132}}</ref> The building was left in disrepair,<ref name="Detwiller p. 15" /> much to the consternation of Boston's [[Puritans]] who disapproved of the theatrical shows.<ref name="Amadon p. B11" /><ref name="BLC p. 13">{{Harvnb|Boston Landmarks Commission|1994|ps=.|page=13}}</ref>

==== Post-Revolutionary use ====
[[File:Faneuilhall104.JPG|thumb|left|Faneuil Hall in 1776]]

Town meetings and gatherings at Faneuil Hall did not resume until about 1777, the year after the U.S. declared independence.<ref name="Brown p. 132" /> The building was sometimes used for celebrations honoring dignitaries and figures allied with the [[Continental Army]], including [[Charles Henri Hector, Count of Estaing|Count Charles Henri Hector of Estaing]], [[Marquis de Lafayette]], [[George Washington]], and French troops.<ref name="BLC p. 13" /><ref name="Quincy pp. 28–29">{{Harvnb|Quincy|2003|ps=.|pages=28–29}}</ref> The building was also used for state lotteries and banquets.<ref name="Brown p. 133" /> The market underneath also saw increased use, being designated as the town's produce and meat market in 1783.<ref name="Brown pp. 149–150">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|pages=149–150}}</ref> The selectmen awarded several contracts for repairs to Faneuil Hall during the early 1780s, including new seats, upgraded windows, and repainting. Doors were removed from the first-floor marketplace, and portraits and [[Bust (sculpture)|busts]] were commissioned for the upper floors.<ref name="Detwiller p. 16">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=16}}</ref> Exterior repairs also took place during that decade.<ref name="Detwiller p. 17" /> Further modifications were contemplated in the 1790s, including roof repairs and interior repainting,<ref name="Detwiller p. 18">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=18}}</ref> as well as the 1791 construction of the Shambles, a rudimentary marketplace annex with extra stalls.<ref name="Quincy p. 38">{{Harvnb|Quincy|2003|ps=.|page=38}}</ref>

Though Faneuil Hall remained in use primarily as a town hall and market,<ref name="BLC p. 13" /><ref name="Quincy p. 29">{{Harvnb|Quincy|2003|ps=.|page=29}}</ref> it found additional uses in the late 18th century, such as military drills,<ref name="Detwiller p. 17" /> worship services for Black residents of Boston,<ref name="BLC p. 13" /><ref name="Detwiller p. 17" /> meetings of the [[Massachusetts Historical Society]],<ref name="Brown p. 134">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|page=134}}</ref><ref name="Amadon p. B12">{{Harvnb|Amadon|1970|ps=.|page=B12}}</ref> and New Year's parties held by French residents.<ref name="Detwiller p. 17" /> The ground-floor market continued to attract merchants from all over the Boston area,<ref name="Amadon p. B12" /><ref name="BLC p. 14">{{Harvnb|Boston Landmarks Commission|1994|ps=.|page=14}}</ref> growing in popularity after the opening of the [[West Boston Bridge]] in 1793.<ref name="Brown p. 151">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|page=151}}</ref> Many merchants participated in Faneuil Hall's events, sometimes marching in honor of events taking place there.<ref name="p2493356849">{{Cite magazine
|title=Beef Before Baubles|work=Historic Preservation|volume=10|issue=1|date=January 1, 1958|pages=26–28|id={{pq|2493356849}}}}</ref> The market was overcrowded by the end of the century, prompting the selectmen to study the feasibility of excavating a cellar in 1799,<ref name="BLC p. 14" /><ref name="Detwiller p. 19">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=19}}</ref> but nothing was done at the time.<ref name="Quincy p. 30">{{Harvnb|Quincy|2003|ps=.|page=30}}</ref> The town meetings were also becoming overcrowded and sometimes had to be moved to the [[Old South Meeting House|Old South Church]].<ref name="Quincy p. 29" /><ref name="BLC p. 14" />

=== 19th century ===
==== 1800s to 1820s ====
The roof was leaking by 1802, and the marketplace stalls were extended the next year.<ref name="Detwiller p. 19" /> Bulfinch was selected as the chairman of the board of selectmen in March 1805,<ref name="Detwiller p. 19" /> and the selectmen formed a committee to evaluate alternatives for the building.<ref name="Amadon p. B14">{{Harvnb|Amadon|1970|ps=.|page=B14}}</ref><ref name="Brown p. 153">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|page=153}}</ref> That May, he presented plans for an expansion of Faneuil Hall.<ref name="Brown p. 153" /><ref name="Detwiller p. 22">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=22}}</ref><ref name="Place p. 124">{{Harvnb|Place|1925|ps=.|page=124}}</ref> Bulfinch, who had experience designing other buildings such as the new [[Massachusetts State House|State House]], was selected to design the expansion,<ref name="BLC p. 14" /><ref name="Brown p. 154">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|page=154}}</ref> with Jonathan Hunnewell as the master mason.<ref name="Quincy p. 30" /><ref name="Detwiller p. 22" /> Bulfinch added a third floor and attic, and he doubled the north–south width,<ref name="BLC p. 14" /><ref name="Alper s634">{{cite book |last=Alper |first=M. Victor |url=https://www.archive.org/details/americasfreedomt0000alpe/page/53 |title=America's Freedom Trail: A Tour Guide to Historical Sites of the Colonial and Revolutionary War Period |date=1976 |publisher=Collier Books |isbn=978-0-02-097150-4 |page=53 |access-date=January 25, 2026|url-access=registration}}</ref><ref name="Place p. 122">{{Harvnb|Place|1925|ps=.|page=122}}</ref> extending Smibert's existing design northward.<ref name="Brown p. 154" /><ref name="Quincy p. 30" /> The third-story facade was decorated with [[pilaster]]s and [[entablature]]s, complementing similar decorations in the stories below, and a new [[gable roof]] was built.<ref name="Amadon p. B15">{{Harvnb|Amadon|1970|ps=.|page=B15}}</ref><ref name="Brown p. 155">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|page=155}}</ref> The open [[Arcade (architecture)|arcades]] on the ground story were enclosed,<ref name="Amadon p. B15" /><ref name="Brown p. 155" /><ref name="NPS p. 6">{{harvnb|National Park Service|1966|ps=.|page=6}}</ref> and the cupola was moved to the east end of the new roof.<ref name="Amadon p. B15" /><ref name="Detwiller p. 232">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=23}}</ref> Inside, a gallery was built above the assembly hall,<ref name="Brown p. 154" /><ref name="NPS p. 6" /> providing seating for women, who could not vote on town matters.<ref name="Quincy p. 31">{{Harvnb|Quincy|2003|ps=.|page=31}}</ref> A new headquarters for the Ancients was built on the fourth floor.<ref name="Place p. 124" /><ref name="Amadon p. B15" /> The final design contains several deviations from Bulfinch's plans, including the placement of entrances and interior spaces.<ref name="Detwiller pp. 23–25">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|pages=23–25}}</ref> The work was completed in March 1806.<ref name="Place p. 124" /><ref name="Brown p. 155" /> The project had cost $56,692,{{efn-ur|Equivalent to ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=56692|start_year=1806|r=-3|fmt=c}} in {{Inflation/year|index=US-GDP}}{{Inflation/fn|index=US-GDP}}}}<ref name="n190068991" /><ref name="Brown p. 155" /> and the debt was not repaid for several years.<ref name="Detwiller p. 232" /> Faneuil Hall largely retained its original design for most of the century.<ref name="n190068991" /><ref name="Brown p. 165">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|page=165}}</ref>

[[File:Heraldic eagle, Faneuil Hall, Boston.jpg|thumb|Eagle statue in the Great Hall, installed 1824]]

The expanded building had cellars available for rent; a market; and offices for the selectmen, [[Assessor (law)|assessors]], the [[Boston Board of Health]], and the [[treasurer]].<ref name="Detwiller p. 232" /><ref name="p89453144">{{Cite magazine |date=Sep 1806 |title=Description of Faneuil Hall. |work=The Literary Magazine, and American Register |page=205 |volume=6 |issue=36 |id={{pq|89453144}}}}</ref> The selectmen met on the second floor, and meetings were hosted in the expanded assembly room, the Great Hall.<ref name="Amadon p. B16">{{Harvnb|Amadon|1970|ps=.|page=B16}}</ref><ref name="Detwiller p. 25">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=25}}</ref> Faneuil Hall shortly began accommodating a variety of events, including anti-British protests before and during the [[War of 1812]], along with parties and [[Independence Day (United States)|Independence Day]] celebrations.<ref name="Brown pp. 155–157">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|pages=155–157}}</ref> The clerk of Faneuil Hall's market was given his own office in 1813, and a residents' patrol group was allowed to convene there starting in 1816.<ref name="Detwiller p. 25" /> During the late 1810s and early 1820s, the selectmen authorized several renovations to the interior and exterior.<ref name="Detwiller p. 26">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=26}}</ref> A bust of President [[John Adams]] was installed in the Great Hall in 1818, and a stone eagle was installed there in 1824.<ref name="Detwiller pp. 27–28">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|pages=27–28}}</ref> Meanwhile, the marketplace had again become overcrowded,<ref name="Amadon p. B16" /> and conditions in the marketplace had become unsanitary.<ref name="Quincy p. 38" /> An early-1820s proposal to extend the building eastward was never carried out,<ref name="Quincy p. 38" /><ref name="Detwiller p. 26" /> and plans for further refurbishments in 1821 were delayed.<ref name="Detwiller p. 28">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=28}}</ref>

Faneuil Hall was used for [[town meeting]]s until 1822,<ref name="Amadon p. B16" /><ref>{{Cite web |date=September 20, 2019 |title=Faneuil Hall History {{!}} Boston Freedom Trail History |url=https://www.bostonteapartyship.com/faneuil-hall |access-date=November 1, 2021 |website=Boston Tea Party Ships |language=en-US |archive-date=November 1, 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20211101203359/https://www.bostonteapartyship.com/faneuil-hall |url-status=live }}</ref> when Boston became a city and the board of selectmen was replaced by the [[Boston City Council]].<ref name="Brown p. 158">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|page=158}}</ref><ref name="Quincy pp. 34–35">{{Harvnb|Quincy|2003|ps=.|pages=34–35}}</ref> The newly established city charter prevented Faneuil Hall from being sold or leased,<ref name="n190194809">{{Cite news |date=January 25, 1923|title=Repairs for Faneuil Hall|via=Newspapers.com|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/springfield-weekly-republican-repairs-fo/190194809/|access-date=January 30, 2026|work=Springfield Weekly Republican|pages=7}}</ref> instead preserving it for "the free use of the people".<ref name="Amadon p. B16" /><ref name="BLC p. 15">{{Harvnb|Boston Landmarks Commission|1994|ps=.|page=15}}</ref> Mayor [[Josiah Quincy III]] advocated the construction of an entirely new marketplace to the east, [[Quincy Market]], which was constructed between 1824 and 1826.<ref name="Quincy p. 28" /><ref name="Snyder i6812" /> All of Faneuil Hall's merchants were relocated to the new marketplace in 1827.<ref name="BLC p. 14" /><ref name="Amadon p. B17">{{Harvnb|Amadon|1970|ps=.|page=B17}}</ref> The same year, [[Alexander Parris]], who had built the Quincy Market buildings, was hired to renovate Faneuil Hall.<ref name="Detwiller p. 232" /> Parris's plans included renovating the first floor and basement; he also planned a westward extension of the building, which was never built.<ref name="Detwiller p. 232" /> The first floor was subdivided into eight storefronts,<ref name="BLC p. 14" /><ref name="Amadon p. B17" /> and the exterior was repainted.<ref name="Detwiller p. 30">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=30}}</ref>

==== 1830s to early 1890s ====
[[File:Faneuil Hall, Boston, 1839.png|thumb|1839 engraving of Faneuil Hall]]

Following the 1820s renovations, the building's tenants included goods stores, a [[poorhouse]], and (on the upper floors) military groups.<ref name="Detwiller p. 30" /> Although Faneuil Hall was no longer used as a marketplace or a town meetinghouse, it was still a popular place for speeches and other events.<ref name="BLC p. 14" /><ref name="Amadon p. B16" /> Any event could use the Great Hall if 50 registered voters signed a petition supporting it.<ref name="BLC p. 15" /> Speeches were originally limited to local matters but were gradually expanded to cover a wider range of topics;<ref name="BLC p. 15" /> during the mid-19th century, speeches on abolition were particularly prominent.<ref name="Detwiller p. 31">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=31}}</ref> Notable speakers there included [[John Quincy Adams]], [[Jefferson Davis]], [[Frederick Douglass]], and [[William Lloyd Garrison]].<ref name="Maloy h872" /> The building also hosted balls, eulogies, military drills, and receptions.<ref name="BLC p. 15" /><ref name="Amadon p. B18" /> Parties at Faneuil Hall were hosted for such figures as President [[Andrew Jackson]]; [[François d'Orléans, Prince of Joinville]]; and [[Alexander Baring, 1st Baron Ashburton]].<ref name="Amadon p. B18" /><ref name="Brown p. 159">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|page=159}}</ref> Faneuil Hall was a popular meeting place for mechanics' groups,<ref name="Detwiller p. 30" /> with the [[Massachusetts Charitable Mechanic Association]] holding fairs there from 1837 onward.<ref name="Amadon p. B18" /><ref name="Bostonian Society 1963">{{cite book |last=Bostonian Society |first= |url=https://www.google.com/books/edition/Proceedings_of_the_Bostonian_Society_Ann/TYPjAAAAMAAJ?hl=en&gbpv=1&bsq=faneuil+hall+Massachusetts+Charitable+Mechanic+Association+1837&dq=faneuil+hall+Massachusetts+Charitable+Mechanic+Association+1837&printsec=frontcover |title=Proceedings of the Bostonian Society, Annual Meeting |date=1963 |publisher=Bostonian Society |page=46 |access-date=January 27, 2026}}</ref> A footbridge connected Faneuil Hall to Quincy Market's central building, allowing events to be held across both buildings.<ref name="Detwiller p. 30" /><ref name="Amadon p. B18" /> Faneuil Hall also sometimes served as an emergency accommodation for displaced people or agencies.<ref name="Brown p. 159" />

With Faneuil Hall being increasingly busy, Parris's protege [[Gridley J. F. Bryant]] proposed upgrading the Great Hall in the 1840s, and a rostrum of his design was subsequently installed there.<ref name="Detwiller p. 31" /> A clock was added to the Great Hall in 1850,<ref name="Detwiller pp. 31–32">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|pages=31–32}}</ref><ref name="Amadon p. B19">{{Harvnb|Amadon|1970|ps=.|page=B19}}</ref> and [[George Healy]]'s painting of the [[Webster–Hayne debate]] was mounted there around that time.<ref name="Detwiller pp. 31–32" /><ref name="BLC p. 5" /> Portraits were installed in that room.<ref name="Detwiller p. 33">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=33}}</ref> The Great Hall had been illuminated by a single chandelier until the early 1850s, when sconces were installed at gallery level. Bryant also proposed expanding the Great Hall into the attic space; this was not carried out, although several rooms were repaired for the Ancients' use.<ref name="Detwiller p. 32">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=32}}</ref> A stair to the rostrum was built as well.<ref name="Detwiller p. 33" /> Market operations at the original Faneuil Hall resumed in 1858.<ref name="BLC p. 15" /><ref name="Amadon p. B18">{{Harvnb|Amadon|1970|ps=.|page=B18}}</ref> Meat merchants became the primary tenants of the original Faneuil Hall,<ref name="Amadon p. B18" /> which continued until the 1970s.<ref name="BLC p. 15" />

A new bell, installed in Faneuil Hall's cupola in 1867,<ref name="Amadon p. B19" /><ref name="Brown p. 166">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|page=166}}</ref> was used by [[Fire lookout|lookouts]] to report fires in northern Boston.<ref name="Detwiller p. 40">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=40}}</ref> Following the [[Great Boston Fire of 1872]], the building hosted a post office for three months.<ref name="n192065957">{{Cite news |date=February 8, 1873 |title=Our Post Office |url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/new-england-farmer-our-post-office/192065957/ |access-date=February 24, 2026 |work=New England Farmer |pages=2 |via=Newspapers.com}}</ref> The building was repainted in the early 1870s, and a new rostrum and candelabras were likely installed in the Great Hall around that time.<ref name="Detwiller p. 34">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=34}}</ref> Faneuil Hall had been identified as a fire hazard as early as 1875, though nothing happened for two decades other than the relocation of some paintings.<ref name="Detwiller p. 39">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=39}}</ref> The cupola was leaning by 1894,<ref name="Detwiller p. 39" /><ref name="n190145146">{{Cite news |date=September 18, 1894|title=Leaning Cupola of Faneuil Hall|via=Newspapers.com|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-boston-globe-leaning-cupola-of-faneu/190145146/|access-date=January 30, 2026|work=The Boston Globe |issn=0743-1791|pages=4}}</ref> and Boston's fire commissioner had directed that the lookout stop ringing Faneuil Hall's bell.<ref name="Detwiller p. 40" /> The old plaster and wood were decaying as well.<ref name="Brown p. 165" /> The city's building commissioner John Damrell said in July 1894 that Faneuil Hall needed to be fireproofed (but that the cupola was "strong and not decaying"),<ref name="p497882800">{{Cite news
|id={{Pq|497882800}}|title=Condition of Faneuil Hall|page=10|work=Boston Daily Globe |issn=0743-1791|date=July 6, 1894}}</ref> and Damrell wrote in a report the next year that the building was vulnerable to fire.<ref name="Detwiller p. 41">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=41}}</ref>

==== 1890s fireproofing ====
By 1898, the building commissioner's office had [[Condemned property|condemned]] the property as a "fire trap" four times in fourteen years. Although an appropriation to fix the property had been pending since 1892, the City Council had not approved it;<ref name="NYT e473">{{cite web |date=February 13, 1898 |title=Faneuil Hall a "Fire Trap."; Agitation in Boston for Needed Changes to the Historical Building. |url=https://www.nytimes.com/1898/02/13/archives/faneuil-hall-a-fire-trap-agitation-in-boston-for-needed-changes-to.html |access-date=January 27, 2026 |website=The New York Times |issn=0362-4331}}</ref> the attic was also sagging.<ref name="p576009702" /><ref name="Detwiller p. 35">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=35}}</ref> The Ancients, which were forced to relocate elsewhere within the building,<ref name="Detwiller p. 35" /> expressed concerns about the flammable meat storage and the lack of emergency exits.<ref name="n189906884">{{Cite news |date=December 29, 1896 |title=Want Faneuil Hall Fireproof |via=Newspapers.com|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/boston-evening-transcript-want-faneuil-h/189906884/ |access-date=January 27, 2026 |work=Boston Evening Transcript |pages=3}}</ref> Historical societies and other local organizations advocated for Faneuil Hall's renovation, citing the building's historical significance.<ref name="n190061501">{{Cite news |date=April 17, 1897|title=Preservation of Faneuil Hall|via=Newspapers.com|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-boston-globe-preservation-of-faneuil/190061501/|access-date=January 29, 2026|work=Boston Daily Globe |issn=0743-1791|pages=8}}</ref><ref name="n190061565">{{Cite news |date=April 21, 1897|title=Patriotic-Historic Orders|via=Newspapers.com|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/boston-evening-transcript-patriotic-hist/190061565/|access-date=January 29, 2026|work=Boston Evening Transcript|pages=11}}</ref> The idea of rebuilding Faneuil Hall was finally taken seriously after the fatal [[Merrimac Street fire]] of 1898.<ref name="n190066297">{{Cite news |date=February 11, 1898|title=Passed the $31,000 Mark|via=Newspapers.com|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/boston-evening-transcript-passed-the-31/190066297/|access-date=January 29, 2026|work=Boston Evening Transcript|pages=7}}</ref> The City Council ultimately allocated $103,000 for reconstructing Faneuil Hall with fireproof material.{{efn-ur|Equivalent to ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=103000|start_year=1898|r=-3|fmt=c}} in {{Inflation/year|index=US-GDP}}{{Inflation/fn|index=US-GDP}}}}<ref name="Brown p. 165" /><ref name="Detwiller p. 43">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=43}}</ref>{{Efn|Sources disagree on whether $1,500 ({{Inflation|value=1500|start_year=1898|index=US|r=-3|fmt=eq}})<ref name="Brown p. 165" /> or $15,000 ({{Inflation|value=15000|start_year=1898|index=US|r=-3|fmt=eq}}) was spent on == Architecture ==
The original building was designed by artist [[John Smibert]]<ref name="NPS p. 3" /><ref name="Brown p. 87" /> and was the only building he ever designed, as Smibert was generally a painter.<ref name="Amadon p. B6" /> The current configuration dates to an 1806 renovation designed by architect [[Charles Bulfinch]],<ref name="Place p. 122" /><ref name="Detwiller p. 232" /> who expanded the footprint to {{Convert|80|by|102|ft}}.<ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.32">{{harvnb|National Park Service|2015|ps=.|page=7.32}}</ref> The exterior [[Form (architecture)|form]] remains largely unchanged from Bulfinch's expansion.<ref name="Detwiller p. 232" /> The only remaining portions of Smibert's original design are parts of the eastern and southern walls and a safe that survived the 1761 fire.<ref name="Place p. 122" /> As expanded by Bulfinch in 1806, Faneuil Hall measures four stories high, including the attic.<ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.32" /><ref name="BLC p. 2">{{Harvnb|Boston Landmarks Commission|1994|ps=.|page=2}}</ref>

=== Exterior ===
[[File:Faneuil Hall (54960642851).jpg|thumb|The western elevation]]

==== Facade ====
The northern and southern [[Elevation (architecture)|elevations]] of the brick facade are divided vertically into nine [[Bay (architecture)|bays]], while the western and eastern elevations are divided into seven bays.<ref name="Quincy p. 30" /><ref name="BLC p. 2" /> The window and door openings on each elevation are symmetrical.<ref name="BLC p. 2" /> The brickwork is red and is laid in [[Flemish bond]].<ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.32" /> The original 1742 walls, which are partially extant on the southern side of the present building, are slightly darker and rougher than the later brickwork.<ref name="Amadon p. B15" /><ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.32" /><ref name="Detwiller p. 60" /> Other decorations are painted a cream color. There is a commemorative plaque, dated 1930, on the eastern elevation near the southwestern corner.<ref name="BLC p. 3">{{Harvnb|Boston Landmarks Commission|1994|ps=.|page=3}}</ref>

The first-story openings are arched<ref name="Detwiller p. 60" /><ref name="BLC p. 3" /> and contain [[sash window]]s from Bulfinch's expansion, when the formerly-outdoor marketplace was placed indoors.<ref name="BLC p. 3" /> The [[Springer (architecture)|springers]] of the arches originally had [[Impost (architecture)|impost]] blocks, but the imposts were removed from alternating windows in the 1827 renovation.<ref name="Detwiller p. 60" /> On the primary or eastern elevation, all except the outermost bays have paneled doors; the second-outermost bays have doors leading to the first floor, while the three center bays lead to the grand staircase. On the western elevation facing Congress Street, the entrances are in the second-outermost bays and the middle bay.<ref name="BLC pp. 2–3">{{Harvnb|Boston Landmarks Commission|1994|ps=.|pages=2–3}}</ref><ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.33" /> As built, the entrances were flanked by lanterns on wrought iron brackets.<ref name="n190065637" /><ref name="Brown p. 167" /> Another entrance, on the southern elevation,<ref name="BLC p. 3" /> provides disability access.<ref name="NPS d350">{{cite web|title=Visit Historic Faneuil Hall (U.S. National Park Service)|website=NPS.gov Homepage (U.S. National Park Service)|date=January 17, 2026|url=https://www.nps.gov/thingstodo/visit-historic-faneuil-hall.htm|access-date=January 29, 2026|archive-date=November 15, 2025|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20251115130755/https://www.nps.gov/thingstodo/visit-historic-faneuil-hall.htm|url-status=live}}</ref> There are some windows and doorways under the first floor, which overlook the basement. The upper stories contain sash windows. On the second story, and the western and eastern elevations of the third story, the tops of the windows bear round arches with limestone [[Keystone (architecture)|keystones]].<ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.32" /><ref name="BLC p. 3" /> The keystones have been described as carved busts of English variety star Dolly Bidwell.<ref name="n190065637" /><ref name="Detwiller p. 46">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=46}}</ref> The northern and southern elevations have rectangular sash windows, topped by detached semicircular [[lunette]]s.<ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.32" />

Each bay is separated by a single [[pilaster]], while the extreme ends of each elevation have two pilasters each.<ref name="BLC p. 2" /> The [[Tuscan-style|Tuscan]]-style pilasters on the first story and the [[Doric order|Doric]] pilasters on the second story are part of Smibert's original design and were emulated in Bulfinch's annex. The third story has [[Ionic order|Ionic]] pilasters, which are entirely of Bulfinch's design.<ref name="BLC p. 2" /><ref name="Place p. 123">{{Harvnb|Place|1925|ps=.|page=123}}</ref> The entablatures above each story are designed in the same style as the pilasters below them; the [[entablature]]s above the first-floor windows are made of brownstone, while the second- and third-story entablatures are made of wood. The second-story Doric entablature has [[triglyph]]s, and the Ionic entablature has [[modillion]]s.<ref name="BLC p. 2" /> The entablatures are topped by protruding limestone [[cornice]]s.<ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.32" /> Atop the western and eastern elevations are [[Tympanum (architecture)|tympana]] with an [[architrave]] containing a lunette, along with a cornice decorated with modillions.<ref name="BLC pp. 2–3" /><ref name="NPS (1974) pp. 7.32–7.33" /> These lunettes are flanked by [[porthole]]-like bullseye windows<ref name="BLC pp. 2–3" /> (sometimes described as [[ox-eye window]]s).<ref name="NPS (1974) pp. 7.32–7.33">{{harvnb|National Park Service|2015|ps=.|pages=7.32–7.33}}</ref>

==== Roof ====
[[File:Faneuil Hall, Boston (493567) (11061765625).jpg|thumb|upright|The cupola, with weathervane atop it]]The [[gable roof]] is oriented west–east and is clad in slate shingles, which slope downward to the north and south.<ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.32" /><ref name="Detwiller p. 60">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=60}}</ref> There are [[dormer]] windows protruding from the gable on both sides; each gable is surrounded by a cornice with limestone [[dentil]]s.<ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.32" /> Each dormer has an ox-eye window.<ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.32" /> There are also two chimneys, dating from Bulfinch's expansion.<ref name="Detwiller p. 232" /> In addition, the building had an {{Convert|8|by|10|ft|adj=on}} U.S. flag, which in the 20th century was the largest such flag at a public building in Boston.<ref name="p8432412062">{{Cite news
|id={{Pq|843241206}}|title=Tattered Flag Above Faneuil Can't Be Helped|date=July 16, 1959|pages=4|work=Boston Daily Globe |issn=0743-1791}}</ref>

Above the roof is a [[cupola]], which was originally located at the center of the roof before being moved to the eastern end during the 1806 renovation.<ref name="Amadon p. B15" /><ref name="Detwiller p. 232" /> The original wooden cupola was rebuilt in steel during the late 1890s renovation.<ref name="n190065171" /><ref name="n190063149" /> The lower part of the cupola is a square tower with louvers on its western elevation and windows on its other elevations.<ref name="BLC p. 2" /> The upper part of the cupola is a [[Bell tower|belfry]], which contains a bell dating from 1867.<ref name="Amadon p. B19" /><ref name="Brown p. 166" /><ref name="BLC p. 2" />{{Efn|One source cites the bell as dating from 1866,<ref name="Viser c101" /> which is the year it was cast.<ref name="Brown p. 166" />}} The bell's clapper ceased to function at the end of [[World War II]] in 1945 (though it had been manually rung several times after that with a mallet). The bell began ringing again in 2007 when the stuck clapper was freed and lubricated and new bellrope attached to the bell.<ref name="Viser c101">{{cite web |title=It tolls for the city |website=The Boston Globe |issn=0743-1791 |first=Matt |last=Viser |date=May 4, 2007 |url=http://archive.boston.com/news/local/articles/2007/05/04/it_tolls_for_the_city/ |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20070614141130/http://www.boston.com/news/local/articles/2007/05/04/it_tolls_for_the_city/ |archive-date=June 14, 2007 |url-status=live |access-date=January 25, 2026}}</ref>

=== Interior ===
Inside Faneuil Hall are many paintings and sculpture busts of Revolutionary War activists, pre–Civil War abolitionists, and political leaders.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.nps.gov/bost/planyourvisit/upload/FH_ART.pdf |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20100911032511/http://www.nps.gov/bost/planyourvisit/upload/FH_ART.pdf |url-status=dead |archive-date=September 11, 2010 |title=Art in Faneuil Hall, Boston National Historical Park Brochure |access-date=May 30, 2018}}</ref> The building's Great Hall contains a series of portraits and a [[George Healy]] painting from 1850; the portraits are generally replicas of works held by the [[Museum of Fine Arts, Boston|Museum of Fine Arts]], while Healy's original painting remains ''in situ''.<ref name="Detwiller p. 65">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=65}}</ref><ref name="BLC p. 5">{{Harvnb|Boston Landmarks Commission|1994|ps=.|page=5}}</ref> Throughout the building, floors are generally made of marble and cement, and stair columns are made of steel and cement.<ref name="Brown p. 167" /> The interior spaces are connected by an elevator, making the public spaces disability-accessible.<ref name="NPS d350" /> During the 1890s, a heating coil and a fan were installed, supplying air throughout the building.<ref name="n190065637" /><ref name="Detwiller p. 47" />

==== Lower stories and staircase ====
The basement level is surrounded by a stone [[Foundation (engineering)|foundation]] wall, which has been covered with plaster in many places. [[Icebox|Cold storage]] rooms are situated outside the wall at each corner.<ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.33" /> The rest of the basement is used for offices and education.<ref name="BLC p. 3" /><ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.33" />

The first (ground) floor contains the market area, a space measuring {{Convert|76|by|100|ft}} across. This space has a concrete floor covered with terrazzo tiles, brick walls, cast-iron and concrete supports, and a ceiling of brick [[Vault (architecture)|vaults]]. The north and south walls each have a west–east array of concrete [[Pier (architecture)|piers]], flanking three west–east arrays of [[cast iron]] columns, which divide the space into bays. There are stalls at the center and the north and south walls, creating an H-shaped floor plan. The west and east walls have [[Colonial Revival architecture|Colonial Revival]]-style entrances.<ref name="BLC p. 4">{{Harvnb|Boston Landmarks Commission|1994|ps=.|page=4}}</ref> The southeast corner has a glass-and-wood booth or stall decorated in the Colonial Revival style.<ref name="BLC p. 4" /><ref name="Detwiller p. 64">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|page=64}}</ref> In the late 20th century, the market area retained some vestiges of its original use, including [[meat hook]]s and signage.<ref name="Detwiller p. 64" />

From the eastern entrance, a wide granite staircase rises to the Great Hall's second-story entrance,<ref name="n190065637" /><ref name="Brown p. 167" /> with two intermediate landings.<ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.33">{{harvnb|National Park Service|2015|ps=.|page=7.33}}</ref> Above that, the staircase continues to the fourth story, with landings stacked atop each other. The staircase is illuminated by lamps attached to cast-iron support columns.<ref name="BLC p. 4" /><ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.33" /> The staircase's current cast-iron construction dates to the 1898–1899 renovation.<ref name="BLC p. 4" /><ref name="Detwiller p. 64" /> Most of the doorways leading off the staircases are designed in the [[Greek Revival architecture|Greek Revival]] style and date from the 1820s renovation, except the doors to the Great Hall and the Ancients' anterooms, which were added by Bulfinch. The top of the stairs has [[fanlight]]s designed by Bulfinch.<ref name="BLC p. 4" />

==== Great Hall ====
[[File:1 faneuil hall meeting hall 2010.JPG|thumb|View of the Great Hall toward the west]]
The second floor is primarily occupied by the lower level of the neoclassical-style Great Hall,<ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.33" /> measuring {{Convert|28|ft}} high and {{Convert|76|by|76|ft}} across.<ref name="Detwiller p. 22" /><ref name="Place p. 123" /><ref name="BLC p. 5" /> This space, fitting 856 occupants,<ref name="p3144306975" /> is accessed from the main stairway via a set of paneled doors designed by Bulfinch.<ref name="BLC p. 4" /><ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.33" /> There is a rostrum on the west wall, while the room's other three sides are surrounded by a third-floor gallery.<ref name="BLC p. 5" /> The columns supporting the galleries are replicas of the original Doric-style columns installed by Bulfinch.<ref name="Detwiller p. 65" /> Beneath the gallery, the pilasters on the second floor's outer walls are paneled<ref name="BLC p. 5" /> and are designed in the Ionic order.<ref name="Amadon p. B26" /><ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.33" /> Between the pilasters are the sash windows of the facade.<ref name="BLC p. 5" />

The Great Hall's lower level has a wooden floor.<ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.33" /> The Great Hall has folding seats that flank a central aisle, facing the rostrum.<ref name="BLC p. 5" /> Additional seating is located on the northern and southern sides of the second floor.<ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.33" /><ref name="BLC p. 5" /> The rostrum itself has a wooden stage and oak [[podium]].<ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.33" /> It is flanked by a pair of staircases at the northwest and southeast corners of the room, which lead to the gallery<ref name="BLC p. 5" /><ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.33" /> and have perforated risers for easier ventilation.<ref name="Amadon p. B26" /><ref name="Detwiller p. 45" /> The wall behind the rostrum is decorated with George Healy's oil painting,<ref name="Demeter g905">{{cite book |last=Demeter|first=Richard|title=Irish America: The Historical Travel Guide|publisher=Cranford Press|date=1997|isbn=978-0-9648253-3-8|url=https://www.google.com/books/edition/Irish_America/0CV2AAAAMAAJ|access-date=January 25, 2026|page=175}}</ref>{{Efn|The name of Healy's painting is variously cited as ''Webster Replying to Hayne'',<ref name="BLC p. 5" /> ''Webster's Reply to Hayne'',<ref name="Berg v770">{{cite book | last=Berg | first=Albert Ellery | title=The Drama, Painting, Poetry, and Song: Embracing a Complete History of the Stage; an Exhaustive Treatise on Pictorial Art; a Choice Collection of Favorite Poems, and Popular Songs of All Nations | publisher=P.F. Collier | date=1884 | url=https://www.google.com/books/edition/The_Drama_Painting_Poetry_and_Song/Y5tNAAAAMAAJ?hl=en&gbpv=1&pg=PA548&printsec=frontcover | access-date=January 25, 2026 | page=548}}</ref> or ''Liberty and Union, Now and Forever''.<ref name="Demeter g905" /><ref name="Alper s634" />}} which measures {{Convert|16|by|30|ft}} and depicts the [[Webster–Hayne debate]] and 130 notable American figures.<ref name="BLC p. 5" /><ref name="Webster c326">{{cite book |title=The diplomatic and official papers of Daniel Webster, while Secretary|publisher=Harper|date=1930|url=https://www.google.com/books/edition/The_diplomatic_and_official_papers_of_Da/q-iJcnL9X9QC?hl=en&gbpv=1&pg=PA375&printsec=frontcover|access-date=January 25, 2026|page=375}}</ref> The wall on either side of the painting has decorations such as [[Swag (motif)|swag motifs]], busts, and niches.<ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.33" /> The western wall also has deep openings with terracotta block infill (which once contained windows), in addition to portraits.<ref name="BLC p. 5" /> There are also arches with keystones carved by [[Daniel Reynert]] in the shape of [[cherub]] heads.<ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.33" />

The gallery has more seats overlooking the rostrum.<ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.33" /><ref name="BLC p. 5" /> The columns from the lower level continue through the gallery, supporting an entablature on the ceiling; a paneled balustrade runs between these columns.<ref name="BLC p. 5" /> At the rear (east) end of the Great Hall, the balustrade contains a stone eagle, next to which is a clock<ref name="BLC pp. 5–6">{{Harvnb|Boston Landmarks Commission|1994|ps=.|pages=5–6}}</ref><ref name="NPS (1974) pp. 7.33–7.34">{{harvnb|National Park Service|2015|ps=.|pages=7.33–7.34}}</ref> donated by the children of Boston.<ref name="n190432725">{{Cite news |date=September 28, 1980 |title=Ask the Globe |via=Newspapers.com|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-boston-globe-ask-the-globe/190432725/ |access-date=February 2, 2026 |work=The Boston Globe |issn=0743-1791 |pages=282}}</ref> There are [[Sconce (light fixture)|sconces]] mounted onto the cornice,<ref name="BLC p. 5" /> along with light fixtures hanging from a lotus blossom–shaped [[Escutcheon (furniture)|escutcheon]] in the middle of the ceiling.<ref name="BLC p. 5" /><ref name="Amadon p. B26" />

==== Other upper-story spaces ====
Portraits of diplomat [[Anson Burlingame]] and U.S. Vice President [[Henry Wilson]], a plaque, and a commemorative tablet are displayed on the second floor.<ref name="BLC p. 4" /> There are also small offices on the second floor, leading off the Great Hall and the stair landing.<ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.33" /> These include the superintendent's office (also known as the custodian's room<ref name="Detwiller pp. 64–65">{{Harvnb|Detwiller|1977|ps=.|pages=64–65}}</ref><ref name="Brown p. 168">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|page=168}}</ref>) at the northeast corner, which measures {{Convert|20|by|20|ft}} across.<ref name="BLC p. 6">{{Harvnb|Boston Landmarks Commission|1994|ps=.|page=6}}</ref> The superintendent's office bears Colonial Revival decorations, including a [[fireplace mantel]] and chimney piece.<ref name="BLC p. 6" /><ref name="Detwiller pp. 64–65" /> There is another room on the southeast corner, across the staircase landing.<ref name="Detwiller p. 65" /> Known as the Boston Art Commission and Park Service Room, it has decorations dating from the late 1890s,<ref name="Detwiller p. 65" /> including a smaller brick mantel.<ref name="Brown p. 168" /> There are estrooms for men and women on the second and third floors, respectively.<ref name="Detwiller p. 45" />
[[File:General view - Museum of the Ancient and Honorable Artillery Company of Massachusetts - IMG 6882.JPG|thumb|The Ancient and Honorable Artillery Company's central hall]]
The fourth floor, or attic, is the headquarters of the [[Ancient and Honorable Artillery Company of Massachusetts]].<ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.34">{{harvnb|National Park Service|2015|ps=.|page=7.34}}</ref><ref name="New England Historic Genealogical Society y575">{{cite book |title=New England Ancestors: Newsmagazine of the New England Historic Genealogical Society|publisher=New England Historic Genealogical Society|date=2002|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=kWlmAAAAMAAJ|access-date=January 25, 2026|page=34}}</ref> The center of this floor has a hall measuring {{Convert|48|by|76|ft}} across.<ref name="Detwiller p. 25" /><ref name="BLC pp. 6–7" /> The space measured {{Convert|30|by|76|ft}} across,<ref name="Detwiller p. 22" /><ref name="p89453144" /> but, to accommodate new roof trusses, was expanded by {{Convert|18|ft}} during the 1890s renovation.<ref name="Detwiller p. 46" /> The walls are made of plaster. The northern and southern walls each have seven doors, which originally led to barrel-vaulted artillery storage rooms;<ref name="BLC pp. 6–7">{{Harvnb|Boston Landmarks Commission|1994|ps=.|pages=6–7}}</ref> Bulfinch's plans showed ten such rooms.<ref name="Detwiller p. 25" /> The western wall has a lunette window and, below that, a {{Convert|5|by|7|ft|m|adj=on}} rendering of the [[Great Seal of Massachusetts]].<ref name="BLC p. 6" /> There are benches at the middle of the room,<ref name="BLC p. 6" /> which are made of iron and wood.<ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.34" /> An ornate, gilded [[frieze]] with military decorations runs atop the walls.<ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.34" /><ref name="BLC pp. 6–7" /> The ceiling is made of plaster, with raised moldings creating a grid; the intersections of these moldings contain circles with light bulbs. Four [[dormer]] windows each to the north and south, embedded in the roof, also illuminate the space.<ref name="BLC p. 7">{{Harvnb|Boston Landmarks Commission|1994|ps=.|page=7}}</ref>

East of the Ancient and Honorable Assembly Company's hall, a staircase ascends to a commandery room just below Faneuil Hall's cupola.<ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.34" /><ref name="BLC p. 7" /> The 13 risers in this staircase have embedded plaques with the names of the [[Thirteen Colonies]].<ref name="BLC p. 7" /><ref name="p516039758">{{cite news
|id={{ProQuest|516039758}}|title=Faneuil Hall Offers Graphic View of History: Steps to Freedom|first=Courtney R.|last=Sheldon|date=October 8, 1947|page=2|work=The Christian Science Monitor|issn=0882-7729}}</ref> The commandery room itself measures {{Convert|48|by|14|ft}} and is illuminated by elliptical windows in the [[coved ceiling]] and by a lunette and bullseye windows from the east.<ref name="BLC p. 7" /> On the commandery room's perimeter are pairs of Ionic columns, which support the coved ceiling.<ref name="NPS (1974) p. 7.34" /><ref name="BLC p. 7" />

== Name ==
Peter Faneuil was of [[French Huguenot]] descent,<ref name="NYT b221" /><ref name="Pratt t799">{{cite web |last=Pratt |first=Mark |date=June 18, 2018 |title=Some Seek Faneuil Hall Name Change Because of Slavery Ties |url=https://www.nbcboston.com/news/local/some-seek-faneuil-hall-name-change-because-of-slavery-ties/1965341/ |access-date=January 24, 2026 |website=NBC Boston}}</ref> and there is disagreement over how his name was pronounced.<ref name="p405457817" /> The pronunciation is variously given as {{IPAc-en|ˈ|f|æ|n|əl}} ({{Respell|FAN|el|}}, rhyming with "panel");<ref name="n190248575" /><ref name="p405457817" /> {{IPAc-en|ˈ|f|ʌ|n|əl}} ({{Respell|FUN|el|}}, "funnel");<ref name="n190248575" /> or {{IPAc-en|ˈ|f|æ|n|j|əl}} ({{Respell|FAN|yel|}}, rhyming with "Daniel").<ref name="p405457817">{{Cite news |title=FYI |last=Mulvoy |first=Thomas M Jr. |date=March 10, 2002 |page=2 |work=The Boston Globe |issn=0743-1791 |id={{Pq|405457817}}}}</ref><ref name="Seelye z586">{{cite web |last=Seelye |first=Katharine Q. |date=June 6, 2018 |title=Boston Grapples With Faneuil Hall, Named for a Slaveholder |url=https://www.nytimes.com/2018/06/06/us/faneuil-hall-name-slavery.html |access-date=February 3, 2026 |website=The New York Times |issn=0362-4331}}</ref> Faneuil himself preferred the {{Respell|FAN|el|}} pronunciation,<ref name="Quincy p. 12" /> but residents often pronounced it as three syllables ({{IPAc-en|ˈ|f|æ|n|j|u|w|ə|l}} {{Respell|FAN|u|el}}; similar to "Fan-you'll").<ref name="Quincy p. 12" /><ref name="n190248575" /> ''The New York Times'' wrote in 1923 that "[[Wendell Phillips]] and other Brahmins called it Funel Hall. The uninitiated favor Fannel Hall. The first would seem to be right."<ref name="NYT c0582" />

Faneuil Hall is distinct from the [[Faneuil Hall Marketplace]], which covers not only Faneuil Hall but also the three Quincy Market buildings.<ref name="p3144306975" /> The term "Faneuil Hall" is sometimes used to refer to the Quincy Market buildings; the original building is not always included in this categorization.<ref name="O Malley h415">{{cite web |last=O'Malley |first=Nick |title=Boston's Faneuil Hall Marketplace: Tourist trap, or bad rap? |website=masslive |date=January 14, 2026 |url=https://www.masslive.com/destinations/2026/01/bostons-faneuil-hall-marketplace-tourist-trap-or-bad-rap.html |access-date=January 30, 2026}}</ref> As such, some visitors have associated the name "Faneuil Hall" exclusively with the other three buildings.<ref name="n190440695" /> Over the years, both Faneuil Hall's market and Quincy Market have been referred to as "Faneuil Hall Market".<ref name="n190204068">{{Cite news |date=August 26, 1926 |title='Quincy Market' 100 Years Old Today |via=Newspapers.com |url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-boston-globe-quincy-market-100-yea/190204068/ |access-date=January 30, 2026 |work=Boston Daily Globe |issn=0743-1791 |pages=10}}</ref> To distinguish from Quincy Market, the name "New Faneuil Hall Market" was used for the original Faneuil Hall's market when it reopened in the 1850s.<ref name="Bostonian Society p. 44">{{harvnb|Bostonian Society|1907|ps=.|page=44}}</ref><ref name="Quincy p. 111">{{Harvnb|Quincy|2003|ps=.|page=111}}</ref> The original Faneuil Hall has also been called the "New Faneuil Hall"<ref name="n190219459" /> and nicknamed "the Cradle of Liberty".<ref name="Stark j577" /><ref name="Brown p. 1242" /><ref name="p141814249" />

Although the Faneuil family had sided with the Loyalists during the American Revolutionary War, the name was not changed at that time.<ref name="Quincy p. 29" /> In August 2017, amid heightened media coverage of the [[removal of Confederate monuments and memorials]], the activist group New Democracy Coalition proposed that Faneuil Hall's name be changed because of Faneuil's participation in the [[Slave trade in the United States|American slave trade]].<ref>{{cite web |url=http://wpri.com/2017/08/17/group-calls-for-faneuil-hall-to-be-renamed/ |title=Group calls for Faneuil Hall to be renamed |first=Michelle |last=Gere |date=August 17, 2017 |access-date=May 30, 2018 |publisher=WPRI}}</ref> The next year, activists suggested naming the building for Boston Massacre victim [[Crispus Attucks]],<ref name="Seelye z586" /><ref name="Press s312">{{cite web |agency=Associated Press |title=Faneuil Hall: Boston landmark named after slaveowner faces boycott |website=The Guardian |date=July 31, 2018 |url=https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2018/jul/31/faneuil-hall-boston-boycott-slavery |access-date=January 31, 2026}}</ref> but Mayor [[Marty Walsh]] opposed a renaming.<ref name="Campbell g217" /><ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.bostonglobe.com/magazine/2017/07/18/marty-walsh-into-confession-make-the-making-mayor-marty-walsh-how-marty-walsh-figured-out-god-what-have-gotten-myself/1s12EpEXiBCLfYGtytBmRJ/story.html |title=Marty Walsh has a confession to make |work=The Boston Globe |issn=0743-1791 |access-date=August 1, 2018}}</ref> Additional name change protests followed in the 2020s, which involved activists chaining themselves to the front door and a [[sit-in]].<ref>{{cite web |last=Mason |first=Amelia |date=October 19, 2022 |title=Activists chain themselves to Faneuil Hall in protest of its slaveholding namesake |url=https://www.wbur.org/news/2022/10/19/activists-chain-themselves-to-faneuil-hall-in-protest-of-its-slaveholding-namesake |access-date=February 4, 2026 |website=WBUR News}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |last=McDonald |first=Danny |title=At City Hall, a sit-in to protest the name of a Boston landmark |website=The Boston Globe |issn=0743-1791 |date=January 12, 2023 |url=https://www.bostonglobe.com/2023/01/12/metro/city-hall-sit-in-protest-name-boston-landmark/ |access-date=February 4, 2026}}</ref> Although the City Council voted in 2023 to endorse a potential renaming, ''[[The Economist]]'' wrote that, because Faneuil Hall was a significant site in the American independence and abolitionist movements, there was not as clear a case to rename it, compared with Confederate namesakes.<ref name="The Economist m537">{{cite web |date=November 9, 2023 |title=Boston's "cradle of liberty" was paid for with slavery profits |url=https://www.economist.com/united-states/2023/11/09/bostons-cradle-of-liberty-was-paid-for-with-slavery-profits |access-date=February 4, 2026 |website=The Economist |id={{Pq|2887653522}}}}</ref>

|title=Faneuil Hall offers history and shopping |via=Newspapers.com|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-daily-item-faneuil-hall-offers-histo/189708270/ |access-date=January 24, 2026 |work=The === Events ===
Over the years, Faneuil Hall has been used for a wide variety of events, such as debates, speeches, campaign events,<ref name="Snyder i6812" /> jury trials,<ref name="n190329241" /> celebrations, commemorations, receptions, and balls.<ref name="BLC p. 15" /><ref name="Amadon p. B18" /> Topics such as slavery, suffrage, labor unions, and international relations have been discussed there.<ref name="BLC p. 15" /><ref name="n190329241" /> One newspaper wrote in 1923 that "practically every distinguished American has spoken there",<ref name="n190194809" /> while another said in 1964 that "every issue of great importance has been debated" in the building's halls.<ref name="n190329241" /> Faneuil Hall did not grant absolute free speech; for example, several speeches were forcibly ended early in the 19th century.<ref name="Brown p. 159" /> Some events have been denied permission to use Faneuil Hall, including an 1851 speech by [[Daniel Webster]]<ref name="n190186760">{{Cite news |date=November 6, 1910|title=When Faneuil Hall Was Closed to Daniel Webster|via=Newspapers.com|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-boston-globe-when-faneuil-hall-was-c/190186760/|access-date=January 30, 2026|newspaper=Boston Daily Globe |issn=0743-1791|pages=59}}</ref> and a 1929 meeting commemorating the execution of anarchist duo [[Sacco and Vanzetti]].<ref>{{cite news |title=Refused Faneuil Hall, Group Threatens Suit; Sacco-Vanzetti Committee Is Backed by Civil Liberties Union, Officers Assert.|newspaper=The New York Times |issn=0362-4331|date=August 3, 1929|url=https://www.nytimes.com/1929/08/03/archives/refused-faneuil-hall-group-threatens-suit-saccovenzetti-committee.html|access-date=January 30, 2026}}</ref>

The Great Hall has traditionally hosted speeches from orators on [[Independence Day (United States)|Independence Day]], July 4.<ref name="Detwiller p. 31" /><ref name="Brown p. 157">{{Harvnb|Brown|1900|ps=.|page=157}}</ref> From 1783 to 1809, the Boston Massacre was also commemorated there every March 5.<ref name="Brown p. 157" /> The [[Ford Hall Forum]] also took place at the building until the 1980s,<ref name="BLC p. 16" /> and Faneuil Hall hosted the Gay and Lesbian Town Meeting from 1977 to {{Circa|1996}}.<ref name="NPS n664">{{cite web |title=Gay and Lesbian Town Meeting |url=https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/gay-and-lesbian-town-meeting.htm |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220911082606/https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/gay-and-lesbian-town-meeting.htm |archive-date=September 11, 2022 |access-date=February 4, 2026 |publisher=National Park Service}}</ref> Boston's mayor ordinarily gives their annual State of the City address at the Great Hall.<ref name="n190514470" /> Other events over the years have included:<!-- Please format the date using [[Template:Dts]] and add a [[Wikipedia:Reliable sources|reliable source]] when adding an event. == Impact and legacy<span class="anchor" id="Impact"></span><span class="anchor" id="Reception and landmark designations"></span> ==
=== Reception ===
[[File:Faneuil Hall in May 1973 - Boston MA.jpg|thumb|Faneuil Hall seen in 1973 from across [[Congress Street (Boston)|Congress Street]]]]
The Great Hall has been praised over the years for its symbolism, even while the first-floor marketplace was criticized or ignored.<ref name="Amadon p. B19" /> A writer in 1826 said that "there is no place more distinguished for powerful eloquence than Faneuil Hall", while a writer in 1881 said that "nothing which has profoundly agitated Boston for more than a hundred years has found the old hall silent".<ref name="Amadon p. B19" /> ''The New York Times'' wrote in 1923 that "Faneuil Hall is dear to the Boston heart" and that the building had become synonymous with lively debate.<ref name="NYT c0582" /> ''[[The Springfield Union]]'' called the building's relics "of inestimable value in perpetuating the local color and traditions" of Boston,<ref name="n190189486" /> and a writer for the ''[[The Christian Science Monitor|Christian Science Monitor]]'' said Faneuil Hall had served as "a forum for concerned Bostonians and their leaders" from the outset.<ref name="p511746198" /> During the [[United States Semiquincentennial]], ''[[Smithsonian (magazine)|Smithsonian]]'' magazine wrote that the structure was "a symbol for the tradition of public debate itself".<ref name="Snyder i6812" />

The building itself was also the subject of commentary. In his 1900 history of the building, Abram English Brown wrote that the building was "conspicuous for its simplicity, and disappointing to tourists", although he felt that the enclosure of the first-floor arcade detracted from the overall design.<ref name="Brown p. 155" /> ''The Boston Globe'' said in 1964 that Faneuil Hall and the adjoining market buildings formed "one of the finest urban spaces in America".<ref name="n190334133">{{Cite news |last=Yudis|first=Anthony J.|date=March 14, 1964|title=Emphasis on Restoration|via=Newspapers.com|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-boston-globe-emphasis-on-restoration/190334133/|access-date=February 1, 2026|work=The Boston Globe |issn=0743-1791|pages=8}}</ref> In 1977, the ''Christian Science Monitor'' wrote that Faneuil Hall's "multi-tiered, humanly tiered character" allowed visitors to get actual food at ground level and "food for thought" on the second story.<ref name="p511967377" /> A writer for ''Change Over Time'' said in 2012 that Faneuil Hall and the other market buildings were "an important contribution to the canon of great American architecture".<ref name="Donofrio p. 115">{{harvnb|Donofrio|2012|ps=.|page=115}}</ref>

A commentator for ''The Youth's Companion'' in 1910 described Faneuil Hall as one of three old buildings that Bostonians were most proud of, aside from the [[Old South Meeting House]] and [[Old State House (Boston)|Old State House]].<ref name="p127148057">{{Cite magazine
|title=The Old State-house Restored|last=Bickford|first=C A|work=The Youth's Companion|volume=84|issue=39|date=September 29, 1910|page=III|id={{Pq|127148057}}}}</ref> Faneuil Hall was rated number 4 in "America's 25 Most Visited Tourist Sites" by ''Forbes Traveler'' in 2008.<ref>{{cite news |last=Baedeker|first=Rob|title=America's 25 Most Visited Tourist Sites|work=Forbes Traveler|date=May 5, 2008|url=http://www.forbestraveler.com/best-lists/most-visited-us-tourist-attractions-slide-4.html?partner=wiki|access-date=May 14, 2008|url-status=dead|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090831200212/http://www.forbestraveler.com/best-lists/most-visited-us-tourist-attractions-slide-4.html?partner=wiki|archive-date=August 31, 2009}}</ref> Along with Quincy Market, Faneuil Hall was described in ''[[The Republican (Springfield, Massachusetts)|The Republican]]'' as a tourist trap,<ref name="O Malley h415" /> and online reviews often complained of overcrowding.<ref name="Messier s106">{{cite web |last=Messier|first=Catherine|title=Boston's Faneuil Hall named most crowded attraction in North America. See why|website=The Patriot Ledger|date=June 13, 2025|url=https://www.patriotledger.com/story/news/local/2025/06/13/boston-massachusetts-faneuil-hall-most-crowded-attraction-in-north-america/84162931007/|access-date=January 30, 2026}}</ref>

=== Landmark designations ===
The building was designated a [[National Historic Landmark]] on October 9, 1960;<ref name="National Park Service" /> the landmark report cited Faneuil Hall's architectural, political/government, and social/humanitarian significance.<ref name="NPS p. 3" /> It was added to the [[National Register of Historic Places]] on October 15, 1966,<ref name="National Park Service">{{Cite report |last=National Park Service |title=Listing of National Historic Landmarks by State |page=2 |url=http://www.nps.gov/history/nhl/designations/Lists/MA01.pdf |access-date=December 6, 2010 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121104225304/http://www.nps.gov/history/nhl/designations/Lists/MA01.pdf |url-status=dead |archive-date=November 4, 2012 |via=nps.gov |mode=cs1}}</ref> the day the [[National Historic Preservation Act of 1966]] went into effect.<ref>{{cite book |author=United States. National Park Service|author2=Preservation Press|title=The National Register of Historic Places|publisher=National Park Service.|year=1991|isbn=978-0-942063-21-9|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=u3p1AAAAMAAJ&pg=PR9|page=9}}</ref> Faneuil Hall is one of eight sites in the {{Convert|43|acre|adj=on}} [[Boston National Historical Park]],<ref name="TCLF x237" /> which was designated in 1974.<ref name="n190440695" /><ref name="n200080202">{{Cite news |date=November 9, 1974 |title=Boston National Historical Park created by legislation |url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-herald-news-boston-national-historic/200080202/ |access-date=June 22, 2026 |work=The Herald-News |via=Newspapers.com |pages=31}}</ref> In 1994, the [[Boston Landmarks Commission]] designated the building's exterior and some interior spaces as [[Boston Landmark]]s.<ref name="BLC List">{{Cite web |date=January 2014 |title=List of Designated Boston Landmarks In Numerical Order |url=https://www.cityofboston.gov/images_documents/PETDESIG_JAN2014_tcm3-40308.pdf |publisher=Boston Landmarks Commission |page=4 | access-date=February 14, 2026}}</ref>
