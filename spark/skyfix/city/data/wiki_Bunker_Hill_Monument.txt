The '''Bunker Hill Monument''' is a [[war memorial]] on Breed's Hill in the [[Charlestown, Boston|Charlestown]] neighborhood of [[Boston]], Massachusetts, United States. It commemorates the [[Battle of Bunker Hill]], a major [[American Revolutionary War]] battle that took place there on June 17, 1775. The monument primarily consists of a {{Convert|221|ft|m|adj=mid|-tall}} granite [[obelisk]] at the center of [[Monument Square Park]], within the [[Monument Square Historic District (Charlestown, Boston, Massachusetts)|Monument Square Historic District]]. The obelisk was designed by [[Solomon Willard]] and erected between 1825 and 1843. It is flanked by [[William Wetmore Story]]'s [[statue of William Prescott]] to the south and an entrance building to the north. Designated as a [[National Historic Landmark]], the Bunker Hill Monument is part of the [[Freedom Trail]] and [[Boston National Historical Park]].

The first monument at the site was dedicated in 1794 and honored American soldier [[Joseph Warren]], who was killed in the battle. After the site was placed for sale in 1822, the Bunker Hill Monument Association (BHMA) was formed to acquire and preserve the site. Willard took over the design from [[Loammi Baldwin Jr.]], who drew up the original specifications based on a design that [[Horatio Greenough]] submitted for a [[Architectural design competition|design competition]]. The cornerstone was laid in 1825, but due to insufficient funds, work was halted twice for extended periods between 1829 and 1840. The monument was dedicated on June 17, 1843.

When completed, the Bunker Hill Monument was the United States' largest obelisk. The monument has required ongoing maintenance through the years. A temporary wooden entrance building was built in 1857, and the Prescott sculpture was added in 1881. Following decades of fundraising, a granite entrance building was completed in 1902. The BHMA maintained the monument until 1919, when Massachusetts's [[Metropolitan District Commission (Massachusetts)|Metropolitan District Commission]] (MDC) took over. The monument has been owned and managed by the [[National Park Service]] since 1976.

=== Breed's Hill ===

{{Vanchor|Breed's Hill}} is a {{convert|62|ft|m|-high|adj=mid}} glacial [[drumlin]] in the southern portion of the Charlestown Peninsula,<ref name="NPS p. 7.6">{{harvnb|National Park Service|2015|ps=.|page=7.6}}</ref><ref name="Rines 1920">{{Cite Americana|wstitle=Breed's Hill}}</ref> created 12,000 years ago by retreating glaciers.<ref name="BB p. 3">{{harvnb|Brouillette|Brown|2000|ps=.|page=3}}</ref> After the area was colonized by the English in 1625,<ref name="BB p. 3" /> Breed's Hill was named for the Breed family, early settlers who owned land on the hill's eastern slope.<ref name="BB p. 4">{{harvnb|Brouillette|Brown|2000|ps=.|page=4}}</ref><ref name="SE p. 26">{{harvnb|Snell|Evans|1982|ps=.|page=26}}</ref> The hill was originally connected to the mainland portion of Charlestown (now the separate city of [[Somerville, Massachusetts|Somerville]]) in colonial times by a short, narrow isthmus known as the [[Charlestown Neck]]. In the 19th and early 20th centuries the peninsula's shape and connections to other landforms were significantly altered, with the waters of the [[Charles River]] between [[Cambridge, Massachusetts|Cambridge]] and Charlestown heavily filled in.<ref>Mapping Boston, Krieger, Alex, ed., MIT Press 1999, pp. 16–18.</ref><ref name="NPS p. 7.6" /> By the [[American Revolution]], the south slope of Breed's Hill had a road leading westward.<ref name="BB p. 5">{{harvnb|Brouillette|Brown|2000|ps=.|page=5}}</ref>

The portion of the hill within Monument Square Park slopes fairly steeply.<ref name="p205121089">{{Cite magazine |last=Tarricone |first=Paul |date=Jul 2009 |title=The Battle is Joined |magazine=Lighting Design + Application |pages=36–39 |volume=39 |issue=7 |id={{ProQuest|205121089}}}}</ref> The buildings along the avenue surrounding Monument Square are mostly three-to-four-story houses,<ref name="BB p. 35">{{harvnb|Brouillette|Brown|2000|ps=.|page=35}}</ref> designed in the [[Greek Revival architecture|Greek Revival]], [[Italianate architecture|Italianate]], and [[Second Empire style|Second Empire]] styles starting in the 1840s.<ref name="BB p. 26">{{harvnb|Brouillette|Brown|2000|ps=.|page=26}}</ref> The [[Charlestown High School]] and the former [[Boston Public Library, Charlestown Branch|Charlestown Library]] are also located on the avenue. == Description ==
The obelisk, entrance building, and Prescott statue are located at the center of Monument Square Park.<ref name="TCLF d911" /> They are surrounded by a north–south concrete platform,<ref name="NPS p. 7.7" /> which is constructed of rectangular and triangular slabs.<ref name="Mahlstedt p. 9">{{harvnb|Mahlstedt|1981|ps=.|page=9}}</ref> An iron fence surrounds the base of the obelisk itself, running between granite piers diagonally opposite each of the obelisk's corners. The fence has posts topped by caps and finials, and the cross-bars have [[Greek cross]]es at the bottom and inverted [[scallop]] designs at the top.<ref name="NPS p. 7.8" /> Brick footings are placed under the platform, while curbstone is installed under the obelisk fence.<ref name="Mahlstedt p. 9" />

=== Obelisk ===

H. Cameron writes that the 220-foot figure is given by sources that cite a report by Loammi Baldwin predating the obelisk's construction, while the 221-foot figure is given by later sources.<ref name="Cameron ch. ==== Exterior ====
The [[Foundation (engineering)|foundation]], measuring {{convert|12|ft}} deep and {{convert|50|by|50|ft}} across,<ref name="Warren p. 205" /> is built of six [[Course (architecture)|courses]] (or horizontal layers) of granite.<ref name="Cameron ch. Obelisk">{{harvnb|Cameron|1953|ps=.|loc=chapter "The Obelisk"}}</ref><ref name="n192875751" /> At the base, the obelisk has a square footprint measuring about {{Convert|30|by|30|ft}} across, which tapers to {{Convert|15|by|15|ft}} near the top of the shaft.<ref name="NPS p. 7.8" /><ref name="n193277482" /><ref name="Holley e055">{{cite book |last=Holley |first=Orville Luther |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=zWpCAAAAcAAJ&pg=PA299 |title=The Picturesque Tourist: Being a Guide Through the Northern and Eastern States and Canada ; Giving an Accurate Description of Cities and Villages, Celebrated Places of Resort, Etc |date=1844 |publisher=J. Disturnell |page=220 |access-date=March 6, 2026}}</ref> The shaft's granite is laid horizontally into 78 courses, each measuring {{Convert|2+2/3|ft}} tall.<ref name="NPS g085" /><ref name="Cameron ch. Obelisk" /> The exterior wall has a surface area of {{Convert|27500|ft2}},<ref name="p205121089" /> and its thickness ranges from {{Convert|6|ft}} at the bottom to {{Convert|2|ft}} at the top.<ref name="n192875751" /> There are narrow windows on the northern elevation at regular intervals, and the top of each elevation has a single square window.<ref name="NPS p. 7.8" /> Several {{Convert|150|W|adj=on}} lamps illuminate the exterior at night.<ref name="p199490591" /><ref name="p205121089" />

The shaft is topped by a {{Convert|12|ft|4=-tall|adj=mid}} tall [[pyramid]]al section with another five courses of granite.<ref name="Cameron ch. Obelisk" />{{Efn|The NPS cites a figure of six courses, including the pyramidion.<ref name="NPS g085" />}} Above the topmost course is a [[pyramidion]], a capstone shaped like a four-sided pyramid.<ref name="NPS g085" /><ref name="Cameron ch. Obelisk" /> The pyramidion measures {{Convert|3.5|ft}} high,<ref name="Cameron ch. Obelisk" /> and its pinnacle's faces are sloped at 90-degree angles from each other.<ref name="NPS p. 7.8" />

=== Bunker Hill Lodge ===
[[File:Bunker Hill Monument (36286).jpg|thumb|The Bunker Hill Lodge near the obelisk|alt=A one-story stone structure with a portico]]
The entrance building, known as the Bunker Hill Lodge, is made of granite blocks<ref name="NPS p. 7.7" /><ref name="HV p. 15">{{harvnb|Hardy-Heck-Moore Inc.|Volz & Associates Inc.|2011|ps=.|page=15}}</ref> and dates from 1902.<ref name="HV p. 15" /><ref name="Boston Evening Transcript 1902" /> It is a one-story [[Neoclassical architecture|neoclassical]] building with a rectangular grid of three by four bays,<ref name="NPS p. 7.9">{{harvnb|National Park Service|2015|ps=.|page=7.9}}</ref> measuring {{Convert|50|by|38|ft}} across and {{Convert|19|ft}} tall.<ref name="ce19010629">{{cite news |date=June 29, 1901 |title=Lodge on the Hill |url=https://www.digitalcommonwealth.org/book_viewer/commonwealth:4742d116g#?xywh=60%2C5770%2C3221%2C4032 |access-date=March 9, 2026 |work=Charlestown Enterprise |pages=1, 4 |via=Digital Commonwealth}}</ref>{{Efn|One source cites the floor plan as measuring {{Convert|50|by|48|ft}}.<ref name="n193022042" />}} The facade is made of gray Deer Isle granite.<ref name="ce19010629" /><ref name="n193022042">{{Cite news |date=May 18, 1901 |title=Will Build a New Lodge |via=Newspapers.com |url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/boston-evening-transcript-will-build-a-n/193022042/ |access-date=March 9, 2026 |work=Boston Evening Transcript |pages=5}}</ref> The eastern elevation has an [[Ionic order|Ionic]]-style [[portico]], which supports a [[pediment]] with a carved [[lintel]].<ref name="NPS p. 7.9" /> Beneath the portico are stairs leading to an elaborate [[cast iron]] door with two [[Rosette (design)|rosettes]].<ref name="NPS p. 7.9" /> The northern and southern elevations also have smaller entrances,<ref name="NPS p. 7.9" /> including a ramp leading inside.<ref name="NPS p. 7.8" /> The rest of the facade has [[casement window]]s with slightly-protruding rectangular window frames, and the corners have [[pilaster]]s with undecorated [[Capital (architecture)|capitals]]. The [[entablature]] just below the roofline has [[egg and dart]] decorations.<ref name="NPS p. 7.9" /> During the 20th century, a sign describing the battle was affixed to the exterior.<ref name="p840156045" />

Inside the Bunker Hill Lodge is an entrance vestibule, a central room (sometimes known as the rotunda), and a pair of smaller spaces flanking the rotunda.<ref name="NPS p. 7.9" /><ref name="ce19010629" /><ref name="HV p. 17">{{harvnb|Hardy-Heck-Moore Inc.|Volz & Associates Inc.|2011|ps=.|page=17}}</ref> The vestibule's floor is made of multicolored marble mosaic tiles arranged into various patterns, while its plaster [[vaulted ceiling]] has a central octagonal [[medallion]].<ref name="HV p. 28">{{harvnb|Hardy-Heck-Moore Inc.|Volz & Associates Inc.|2011|ps=.|page=28}}</ref> The rotunda is at the northwestern corner<ref name="NPS p. 7.9" /> and measures {{Convert|12|by|12|ft}} across, with a mosaic-tiled floor.<ref name="ce19010629" /><ref name="HV p. 28" /> The rotunda's Siena-marble walls support a plaster ceiling dome with [[coffers]].<ref name="ce19010629" /><ref name="HV p. 29">{{harvnb|Hardy-Heck-Moore Inc.|Volz & Associates Inc.|2011|ps=.|page=29}}</ref> [[Henry Dexter (sculptor)|Henry Dexter]]'s [[Statue of Joseph Warren (Charlestown, Boston)|large statue]] of Warren, built for an earlier entrance building in 1857,<ref name="BB p. 23" /><ref name="HV p. 16" /> stands in the rotunda.<ref name="NPS p. 7.6" /> Leading off the rotunda to the north and south are {{Convert|11|x|12|ft|adj=on}} rooms, which originally served as men's and women's lounges, respectively.<ref name="ce19010629" /><ref name="HV p. 30">{{harvnb|Hardy-Heck-Moore Inc.|Volz & Associates Inc.|2011|ps=.|page=30}}</ref>

=== Prescott statue ===
[[File:Prescott statue (36283).jpg|left|thumb|upright|The Prescott statue, with inscription at the podium|alt=A bronze statue on a pedestal]]
The bronze [[Statue of William Prescott|William Prescott statue]], south of the obelisk, was sculpted by [[William Wetmore Story]] in 1880–1881. The sculpture is raised on a granite pedestal with the inscription "Colonel William Prescott; June 17, 1775".<ref name="NPS p. 7.9" /><ref name="Ryan c415">{{cite book |last=Ryan |first=Donald R. |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=wKNlEQAAQBAJ&pg=PA197 |title=Colonel William Prescott: Heroic Commander of the Battle of Bunker Hill |date=July 15, 2025 |publisher=Casemate |isbn=978-1-63624-569-0 |page=197}}</ref> This pedestal also contains other panels, which are left blank.<ref name="n193107084">{{Cite news |date=June 18, 1881 |title=The Seventeenth |via=Newspapers.com |url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/boston-evening-transcript-the-seventeent/193107084/ |access-date=March 11, 2026 |work=Boston Evening Transcript |pages=11}}</ref> The sculpture itself stands about {{Convert|8|ft}}<ref name="Ryan c415" /> or {{Convert|9|ft}} tall.<ref name="NPS p. 7.9" /><ref name="n193107084" /> The statue depicts Prescott with his right leg advancing and his right hand grasping a sword, while his left hand is extended backward.<ref name="Ryan c415" /><ref name="n193107084" /> The pose reportedly depicted his appearance as he told his men, "Don't fire until you see the whites of their eyes".<ref name="Kaiser k046" /><ref name="p503757806">{{Cite news |last=Hennessy |first=M E |date=June 17, 1919 |title=Story of a Shrine of American History |via=Newspapers.com |url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-boston-globe-story-of-a-shrine-of-am/193034474/ |access-date=March 9, 2026 |work=The Boston Daily Globe |issn=0743-1791 |page=7  |id={{ProQuest|503757806}}}}</ref> Prescott is sometimes credited with having first said this phrase during this battle; the phrase is variously also attributed to [[Israel Putnam]], [[John Stark]], or [[Richard Gridley]], though in actuality, none of them originated it.<ref>{{cite journal|title=Who Said, "Don't fire till you see the whites of their eyes"?|url=https://allthingsliberty.com/2020/06/who-said-dont-fire-till-you-see-the-whites-of-their-eyes/|journal=Journal of the American Revolution|date=June 17, 2020|first=J. L.|last=Bell|access-date=July 13, 2023|archive-date=July 13, 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230713220716/https://allthingsliberty.com/2020/06/who-said-dont-fire-till-you-see-the-whites-of-their-eyes/|url-status=live}}</ref> The statue also has a broad-brimmed hat and a loose coat,<ref name="n193107084" /> and there are a shovel and pickaxe near its feet.<ref name="Ryan c415" />

=== Background ===
==== Battle ====
{{Main|Battle of Bunker Hill}}
In June 1775, American [[Patriot (American Revolution)|patriots]], having caught word of a [[Kingdom of Great Britain|British]] plan to fortify the Charlestown [[peninsula]], decided to fortify it before the British could.<ref>Hubbard, Robert Ernest.  ''Major General Israel Putnam: Hero of the American Revolution,'' p. 84, McFarland & Company, Inc., Jefferson, NC, 2017.  {{ISBN|978-1-4766-6453-8}}.</ref> On June 16, under the leadership of General [[Israel Putnam]] and Colonel [[William Prescott]], the Americans stole out onto the Charlestown Peninsula to establish defensive positions on the hills overlooking Boston.<ref name="Frothingham pp. 122–123">{{Harvnb|Frothingham|1851|pages=122–123|ps=.}}</ref><ref name="BB p. 7">{{harvnb|Brouillette|Brown|2000|ps=.|page=7}}</ref> Prescott originally intended to build a [[redoubt]], or defensive fortification, on Bunker Hill,<ref name="Ketchum pp. 82-83" /><ref name="Heitert p. 33">{{Harvnb|Heitert|2009|page=33|ps=.}}</ref> but the redoubt was instead constructed on the nearby Breed's Hill, which was lower and was closer to Boston.<ref name="BB p. 7" /><ref name="Frothingham pp. 123–124">{{Harvnb|Frothingham|1851|pages=123–124|ps=.}}</ref><ref name="Rettig Shedd p. 8.2">{{harvnb|Rettig|Shedd|1975|ps=.|page=8.2}}</ref> Early on June 17, the British discovered the fortifications and set out to reclaim the peninsula.<ref name="Frothingham p. 125">{{Harvnb|Frothingham|1851|page=125|ps=.}}</ref> British soldiers under General [[William Howe, 5th Viscount Howe|William Howe]] sent troops to attack Breed's Hill;<ref name="Ketchum p. 139">{{Harvnb|ps=.|Ketchum|1999|page=139}}</ref> colonists held off the first two British attacks, but the third attack forced the rebels to retreat.<ref>{{Cite journal|last=Withington|first=Robert|date=June 1949|title=A French Comment on the Battle of Bunker Hill|journal=The New England Quarterly|volume=22|issue=2|pages=235–240|doi=10.2307/362033|jstor=362033|issn=0028-4866}}</ref>[[File:Array of American Forces on the Field at the Battle of Breeds Hill.png|thumb|A map of the Battle of Bunker Hill|alt=Refer to caption]]The British won at great cost, having lost a significant amount of the officer corps stationed in America.<ref name="BB p. 8">{{harvnb|Brouillette|Brown|2000|ps=.|page=8}}</ref><ref name="Brooks pp. 183-184">{{Cite book |last=Brooks |first=Victor |url=https://archive.org/details/bostoncampaignap0000broo |title=The Boston Campaign |publisher=Combined Publishing |year=1999 |isbn=1-58097-007-9 |pages=183–184 |oclc=42581510 |ref=Brooks |url-access=registration}}</ref> On the American side, 450 were killed or wounded,<ref name="Rettig Shedd p. 8.2" /><ref name="Ketchum pp. 190, 193" /> including [[Joseph Warren]], to whom a memorial was later built on the site.<ref name="BB p. 11" /><ref name="BMJ j174" /> (Prescott, who would later be memorialized as well,<ref name="n193106532" /><ref name="BB p. 28" /> survived the battle.<ref name="NPS b164">{{cite web |date=June 17, 2020 |title=Colonel William Prescott: A Glorious Immortality |url=https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/william-prescott.htm#_ftnref3 |access-date=April 5, 2026 |publisher=U.S. National Park Service}}</ref>) The British recorded more than twice as many casualties, at 1,054.<ref name="Rettig Shedd p. 8.2" /><ref name="Ketchum pp. 190, 193">{{Harvnb|ps=.|Ketchum|1999|pages=190, 193}}</ref> The dead were buried where they lay.<ref name="BB p. 8" /><ref name="Heitert p. 36">{{Harvnb|Heitert|2009|page=36|ps=.}}</ref> While many corpses were reclaimed by families of the deceased,<ref name="Heitert p. 36" /> a 2009 news report stated that mass graves could still exist under gardens around Monument Square.<ref name="p4049548632">{{Cite news |last=MacQuarrie |first=Brian |date=March 8, 2009 |title=Bunker Hill dead may lie under gardens |via=Newspapers.com |url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-boston-globe-bunker-hill-dead-may-li/193422979/ |access-date=March 14, 2026 |work=The Boston Globe |issn=0743-1791 |pages=A1, [https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-boston-globe-bunker-hill-dead-may-li/193423005/ A10]  |id={{ProQuest|404954863}}}}</ref> The British strengthened the fortifications on the hill before [[Evacuation of Boston|evacuating Boston]] in March 1776.<ref name="Heitert p. 33" /><ref name="BB p. 8" /> The area surrounding Breed's Hill's summit was parceled up in the early 19th century.<ref name="BB p. 11">{{harvnb|Brouillette|Brown|2000|ps=.|page=11}}</ref> The site of the Bunker Hill Monument was likely owned by Sarah Russell and several other landowners.<ref name="SE p. 26" /><ref name="BB p. 11" />

==== Early commemoration attempts ====
The Battle of Bunker Hill's anniversary was not commemorated during the ongoing war.<ref name="Frothingham p. 337">{{Harvnb|Frothingham|1851|page=337|ps=.}}</ref> The [[Grand Lodge of Massachusetts]] constructed the first monument commemorating the battle to honor Warren, who had been one of their members.<ref name="BB p. 11" /><ref name="BMJ j174" /> The Warren memorial, delayed due to a lack of money,<ref name="BB p. 11" /><ref name="Frothingham p. 337" /> was dedicated on December 2, 1794.<ref name="BMJ j174" /><ref name="Frothingham p. 338">{{Harvnb|Frothingham|1851|page=338|ps=.}}</ref> It was an {{convert|18|ft|m|adj=on|1}} wooden column designed in the [[Tuscan order]], standing on a brick [[pedestal]] measuring {{Convert|10|ft}} tall and {{Convert|8|by|8|ft}} across.<ref name="BMJ j174">{{cite book |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=oVIBAAAAYAAJ&dq=bunker+hill+monument+1794+Joseph+Warren&pg=PA298 |title=Brooklyn Medical Journal |date=1905 |publisher=Medical Society of Company of Kings |pages=297–298 |access-date=February 24, 2026}}</ref><ref name="Frothingham p. 339">{{Harvnb|Frothingham|1851|page=339|ps=.}}</ref> This pillar, which stood near where Warren was believed to have died,<ref name="BB p. 11" /><ref name="Frothingham p. 339" /> was surrounded with a fence {{Circa|1796}}.<ref name="n193021748">{{Cite news |date=June 17, 1902 |title=Model Monument Wreathed |via=Newspapers.com |url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/boston-post-model-monument-wreathed/193021748/ |access-date=March 9, 2026 |work=Boston Post |pages=8}}</ref> The Warren memorial remained in place until 1825.<ref name="Frothingham p. 339" />

The Charlestown Artillery hosted its first parade memorializing the battle in 1794,<ref name="BB p. 11" /><ref name="Frothingham p. 338" /> and further such parades took place in the early 19th century.<ref name="BB p. 11" /><ref name="Frothingham p. 339" /> Several paintings honoring the battle were also commissioned in the late 18th and early 19th centuries.<ref name="BB p. 11" /> After [[Henry Dearborn]] published a book about the battle in 1818, controversies regarding the book's accuracy (and particularly his criticisms of Putnam's role<ref name="Wright p. 166">{{harvnb|Wright|1953|ps=.|page=166}}</ref><ref name="Warren pp. 31–32">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|pages=31–32}}</ref>) rekindled interest in the topic.<ref name="p509407849" /> Interest in a permanent monument may have been inspired by an 1817 visit to the battlefield by President [[James Madison]].<ref name="Purcell p. 62">{{harvnb|Purcell|2003|ps=.|page=62}}</ref>

=== Origins, early fundraising, and land acquisition ===
The Bunker Hill battlefield was placed for sale at an auction in April 1822.<ref name="BB p. 12">{{harvnb|Brouillette|Brown|2000|ps=.|page=12}}</ref><ref name="p498427221">{{Cite news |last=Rugg |first=Sarah Pomeroy |date=June 17, 1925 |title=Boston Men Started Monument—Women Finished It |via=Newspapers.com |url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-boston-globe-boston-men-started-monu/193179265/ |access-date=March 11, 2026 |work=The Boston Daily Globe |issn=0743-1791 |page=9  |id={{ProQuest|498427221}}}}</ref> This attracted the notice of the businessman [[William Tudor (1779–1830)|William Tudor]],<ref name="Frothingham p. 340">{{Harvnb|Frothingham|1851|page=340|ps=.}}</ref><ref name="Packard pp. 8–9">{{harvnb|Packard|1853|ps=.|pages=8–9}}</ref> who is often credited with devising the idea for the monument.<ref name="p503757806" /> One of his acquaintances, the physician [[John Collins Warren (surgeon, born 1778)|John Collins Warren]], bought part of the site that November,<ref name="BB p. 12" /><ref name="Frothingham p. 340" /> constituting about {{Convert|2+3/4|acre}}.<ref name="Frothingham p. 340" /><ref name="n192875751">{{Cite news |date=June 17, 1893 |title=Silent, Yet It Speaks |via=Newspapers.com |url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-boston-globe-silent-yet-it-speaks/192875751/ |access-date=March 7, 2026 |work=The Boston Daily Globe |issn=0743-1791 |pages=7}}</ref> Subsequently, several men—including Warren, Tudor, [[Daniel Webster]], [[George Ticknor]], William Sullivan, and George Blake—met at the house of [[Thomas Handasyd Perkins]] to discuss plans for a monument.<ref name="Packard p. 9">{{harvnb|Packard|1853|ps=.|page=9}}</ref><ref>{{Harvnb|Frothingham|1851|pages=340–341|ps=}}; {{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|pages=38–39}}</ref> On May 10, 1823, the group called their first meeting<ref name="Packard p. 9" /><ref name="Frothingham p. 341">{{Harvnb|Frothingham|1851|page=341|ps=.}}</ref><ref name="Warren pp. 39–40">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|pages=39–40}}</ref> at Boston's [[Merchants Exchange (Boston)|Merchants Exchange]].<ref name="BB p. 12" /><ref name="Warren pp. 39–40" /> About two dozen attendees formed the Bunker Hill Monument Association (BHMA).  Massachusetts Governor [[William Eustis]] signed an act on June 7, 1823, allowing the BHMA to formally organize as a private [[corporation]].<ref name="BB p. 12" /><ref name="Frothingham p. 341" /><ref name="Packard pp. 9–10">{{harvnb|Packard|1853|ps=.|pages=9–10}}</ref> Eustis's immediate predecessor, [[John Brooks (governor)|John Brooks]], was the organization's first president.<ref name="Frothingham p. 341" /><ref name="Warren p. 49">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|page=49}}</ref><ref name="Wright p. 167">{{harvnb|Wright|1953|ps=.|page=167}}</ref> Only men could become members; women were not given membership until 1936.<ref name="NYT t000">{{cite news |date=January 13, 1936 |title=Admits Women After 113 Years. |url=https://www.nytimes.com/1936/01/13/archives/admits-women-after-113-years.html |access-date=March 13, 2026 |work=The New York Times |issn=0362-4331 |language=en-US |agency=Associated Press}}</ref><ref name="The Daily Bulletin 1936">{{Cite news |last=Williams |first=Charl Ormond |date=November 20, 1936 |title=Modern Women |via=Newspapers.com |url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/telegraph-bulletin-modern-women/193280369/ |access-date=March 13, 2026 |work=The Daily Bulletin |pages=7}}</ref> During its inaugural year, the BHMA mainly focused on administrative matters,<ref name="BB p. 12" /><ref name="Packard p. 11" /> forming the Standing Committee to oversee its operations.<ref name="Wright p. 169">{{harvnb|Wright|1953|ps=.|page=169}}</ref> The new organization also collected Revolutionary-era artifacts, particularly those related to the Battle of Bunker Hill; in so doing, they obtained the Washington Benevolent Society's entire collection.<ref name="Warren p. 194">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|page=194}}</ref>

The BHMA began soliciting donations from the public in mid-1824.<ref name="Packard p. 11" /> The association's director offered honorary memberships to those who donated at least $5,{{efn-ur|Equivalent to ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=5|start_year=1823|r=0|fmt=c}} in {{Inflation/year|index=US-GDP}}{{Inflation/fn|index=US-GDP}}}}<ref name="p498427221" /><ref name="Packard p. 11">{{harvnb|Packard|1853|ps=.|page=11}}</ref><ref name="Frothingham p. 342">{{Harvnb|Frothingham|1851|page=342|ps=.}}</ref> and the BHMA distributed [[Flyer (pamphlet)|flyers]] throughout [[New England]], asking for donations.<ref name="BB p. 12" /><ref name="Frothingham p. 342" /> In its public communications, the BHMA extolled the site's topography and implied that the battlefield would be preserved as an open landscape.<ref name="BB p. 15">{{harvnb|Brouillette|Brown|2000|ps=.|page=15}}</ref> In July 1824, the BHMA appointed a group to oversee preparations for a cornerstone-laying ceremony.<ref name="Warren pp. 123–124">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|pages=123–124}}</ref> The association invited the [[Marquis de Lafayette]] (who was [[Visit of the Marquis de Lafayette to the United States|touring the United States for the war's 50th anniversary]]<ref>{{cite book |last=Levasseur |first=Auguste |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=PN5hEAAAQBAJ |title=Lafayette in America in 1824 to the monument site that August.<ref name="Frothingham p. 342" /><ref name="Warren pp. 80–81">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|pages=80–81}}</ref> He agreed to donate funds and speak at the cornerstone-laying ceremony;<ref name="Frothingham p. 342" /><ref name="Orr 1939">{{Cite news |last=Orr |first=Mary Lincoln |date=June 17, 1939 |title=Bunker Hill Monument Built By Women After the Men Failed |via=Newspapers.com |url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-boston-globe-bunker-hill-monument-bu/193280842/ |access-date=March 13, 2026 |work=The Boston Daily Globe |issn=0743-1791 |pages=9}}</ref> his involvement, along with regular newspaper reports on the progress of the fundraiser, increased public interest in the monument.<ref name="Frothingham p. 343">{{Harvnb|Frothingham|1851|page=343|ps=.}}</ref> In a letter that October, the BHMA's directors suggested that $75,000 be raised.{{efn-ur|Equivalent to ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=75000|start_year=1824|r=-3|fmt=c}} in {{Inflation/year|index=US-GDP}}{{Inflation/fn|index=US-GDP}}}}<ref name="Warren p. 86">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|page=86}}</ref> Early in 1825, Eustis suggested to the [[Massachusetts General Court]], the state legislature, that the state government take over the obelisk following its completion.<ref name="Warren pp. 58–59">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|pages=58–59}}</ref><ref name="Purcell p. 65">{{harvnb|Purcell|2003|ps=.|page=65}}</ref> These suggestions were discarded when he died shortly afterward.<ref name="Warren p. 93">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|page=93}}</ref>

In February 1825, the General Court authorized the BHMA to acquire up to $10,000 of stone hammered at the state prison.{{efn-ur|Equivalent to ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=10000|start_year=1825|r=-3|fmt=c}} in {{Inflation/year|index=US-GDP}}{{Inflation/fn|index=US-GDP}}|name=10000-amount-1825}}<ref name="Warren p. 94">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|page=94}}</ref> The same legislation also allowed the organization to acquire up to {{convert|15|acre|ha|1}} on Breed's Hill.<ref name="n192875751" /><ref name="Packard p. 13">{{harvnb|Packard|1853|ps=.|page=13}}</ref> This area was soon acquired<ref name="Frothingham p. 343" /><ref name="Chisholm">{{Cite EB1911|wstitle=Bunker Hill|volume=4|pages=798–799}}</ref> from ten landowners at approximately {{Convert|1550|$/acre}};{{efn-ur|Equivalent to ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=1550|start_year=1825|r=-3|fmt=c}} per acre or ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=3800|start_year=1825|r=-3|fmt=c}} per hectare in {{Inflation/year|index=US-GDP}}{{Inflation/fn|index=US-GDP}}}} it did not include any of the Breed family's former holdings.<ref name="Warren pp. 101–102">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|pages=101–102}}</ref> Each landowner agreed to sell the BHMA their land at its [[appraised value]], but one of the landowners subsequently demanded ten times the appraised amount of his site. To avoid delays, the association agreed to pay the inflated price.<ref name="p498427221" /><ref name="Packard p. 13" /> The BHMA also wanted to add [[Winthrop Square (Charlestown, Boston)|Charlestown's nearby training field (now Winthrop Square)]] to its land holdings, but this never occurred for financial reasons.<ref name="Warren pp. 195–196">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|pages=195–196}}</ref> About 2,500 donors had given between $5 and $500 each by April 1825.{{efn-ur|Equivalent to ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=5|start_year=1825|r=-1|fmt=c}}–{{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=500|start_year=1825|r=-1|fmt=c}} in {{Inflation/year|index=US-GDP}}{{Inflation/fn|index=US-GDP}}}}<ref name="p498427221" /><ref name="Packard p. 12">{{harvnb|Packard|1853|ps=.|page=12}}</ref>

=== Design and cornerstone laying ===
[[File:Bunker Hill (7201188696).jpg|thumb|The obelisk as seen from the street|alt=People crossing a street. In the background are trees and an obelisk.]]
The BHMA wanted a structure with a design connoting heroism.<ref name="Purcell p. 62" /> [[Solomon Willard]] was appointed in late 1824 to devise plans for a column.<ref name="Wright p. 167" /><ref name="Warren pp. 156–157">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|pages=156–157}}</ref> The BHMA resolved to raise $37,000 for the construction of Willard's design,{{efn-ur|Equivalent to ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=37000|start_year=1825|r=-3|fmt=c}} in {{Inflation/year|index=US-GDP}}{{Inflation/fn|index=US-GDP}}|name=37000-amount-1825}} which Dearborn wrongly believed would be taller than any other commemorative column worldwide.<ref name="Wright p. 167" /> In January 1825, the BHMA decided to host an [[architectural design competition]] for the monument, publishing specifications for the monument in local newspapers,<ref name="Wright p. 167" /><ref name="Warren p. 155">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|page=155}}</ref> and Willard withdrew his plans.<ref name="Wright p. 167" /><ref name="Warren pp. 156–157" /> The first design committee consisted of Webster, the engineer [[Loammi Baldwin Jr.]], George Ticknor, [[Gilbert Stuart]], and [[Washington Allston]].<ref name="BB p. 15" /><ref name="Warren p. 155" /><ref name="Purcell p. 63">{{harvnb|Purcell|2003|ps=.|page=63}}</ref> The committee offered $100 for the best design.{{efn-ur|Equivalent to ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=100|start_year=1825|r=-3|fmt=c}} in {{Inflation/year|index=US-GDP}}{{Inflation/fn|index=US-GDP}}}}<ref name="Cameron ch. Obelisk" /><ref name="BB p. 15" /><ref name="Packard p. 15">{{harvnb|Packard|1853|ps=.|page=15}}</ref> It received 50 formal submissions<ref name="Wright p. 167" /><ref name="BB p. 15" /><ref name="Frothingham p. 343" /> mailed from as far away as [[South Carolina]].<ref name="Warren p. 158">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|page=158}}</ref> Several plans were submitted after the April 1 deadline, including alternatives such as a [[Gothic church]], but were not considered.<ref name="Wright p. 167" /> Although the committee preferred designs for a column,<ref name="Wright pp. 167–168" /><ref name="Warren p. 156">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|page=156}}</ref> [[Robert Mills (architect)|Robert Mills]] and [[Horatio Greenough]] also submitted designs for obelisks.<ref name="Purcell p. 63" /><ref name="Wright pp. 167–168">{{harvnb|Wright|1953|ps=.|pages=167–168}}</ref> Greenough's design consisted of an obelisk with statuary at the base and plain surfaces elsewhere, while Mills's design was more ornate.<ref name="Wright p. 169" /> Debate arose over whether an obelisk or a column should be used.<ref name="Wright pp. 167–168" /><ref name="Purcell pp. 63–64">{{harvnb|ps=.|Purcell|2003|pages=63–64}}</ref> At the time, obelisks were frequently used at graves,<ref name="Purcell pp. 63–64" /> whereas columns were affiliated with heroism.<ref name="Purcell pp. 62–63">{{harvnb|Purcell|2003|ps=.|pages=62–63}}</ref>

The BHMA committee, in late April, suggested that Greenough receive the $100 award.<ref name="Wright p. 169" /><ref name="Warren p. 158" /> On May 19, the association narrowed down the choices to a column and an obelisk, and another committee was appointed to procure designs and tentative budgets for both.<ref name="Frothingham p. 343" /><ref name="Packard p. 15" /> At a vote on June 7, the committee voted 11–5 in favor of an obelisk,<ref name="Warren p. 168">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|page=168}}</ref><ref name="Packard pp. 15–16">{{harvnb|Packard|1853|ps=.|pages=15–16}}</ref> having considered an obelisk more imposing and appropriate for the site.<ref name="Packard p. 16" /> Baldwin, who sat on the board of artists, was tasked with drawing specifications for Greenough's design.<ref name="BB p. 15" /> Dearborn wrote a letter advocating for a column, which he said would cost $58,500,{{efn-ur|Equivalent to ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=58500|start_year=1825|r=-3|fmt=c}} in {{Inflation/year|index=US-GDP}}{{Inflation/fn|index=US-GDP}}}}<ref name="Warren pp. 169–171">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|pages=169–171}}</ref> while Willard claimed that an obelisk would cost only $37,000.{{efn-ur|name=37000-amount-1825}}<ref name="Warren pp. 169–171" /> The final design was adopted July 5,<ref name="Packard p. 16" /><ref name="Wright p. 170">{{harvnb|Wright|1953|ps=.|page=170}}</ref><ref name="Warren p. 183">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|page=183}}</ref> when Baldwin recommended a plan costing $100,000.{{efn-ur|Equivalent to ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=100000|start_year=1825|r=-3|fmt=c}} in {{Inflation/year|index=US-GDP}}{{Inflation/fn|index=US-GDP}}}}<ref name="Wright p. 170" /><ref name="Warren p. 169">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|page=169}}</ref> Baldwin's design deviated from Greenough's original proposal, which had called for a {{Convert|100|ft|m|-tall|adj=mid}} obelisk atop an elevated platform, with buttresses at the corner.<ref name="Warren p. 183" /> Baldwin's plan was austere and sparsely decorated,<ref name="Warren p. 183" /> measuring {{Convert|220|ft}} tall and {{Convert|30|x|30|ft}} across at the base, with the obelisk's faces oriented with the cardinal directions.<ref name="BB p. 16">{{harvnb|Brouillette|Brown|2000|ps=.|page=16}}</ref><ref name="Warren p. 184">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|page=184}}</ref> The BHMA also considered installing statues of soldiers who fought at Bunker Hill but ultimately decided against it.<ref name="n193106532">{{Cite news |date=June 18, 1881 |title=Exercises at the Monument |via=Newspapers.com |url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-boston-globe-exercises-at-the-monume/193106532/ |access-date=March 11, 2026 |work=The Boston Daily Globe |issn=0743-1791 |pages=2}}</ref>

Meanwhile, the BHMA procured a cornerstone with inscriptions and artifacts relating to the battle.<ref name="Warren pp. 138–140">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|pages=138–140}}</ref> The organization also wanted to invite as many Revolutionary War veterans as possible.<ref name="Warren p. 145">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|page=145}}</ref> The cornerstone was laid on June 17, 1825, the battle's 50th anniversary,<ref name="BB p. 15" /><ref name="Warren p. 86" /><ref name="Frothingham p. 344">{{Harvnb|Frothingham|1851|page=344|ps=.}}</ref> at the center of the proposed site.<ref name="Packard p. 16" /><ref name="Cameron ch. Transient Cornerstone">{{harvnb|Cameron|1953|ps=.|loc=chapter "Transient Cornerstone"}}</ref> More than 100,000 attended the event,<ref name="Purcell p. 65" /><ref name="MassMoments June 17, 1825" /> which included a parade and much fanfare.<ref name="p870391600">{{Cite magazine |last=Lockhart |first=Paul |date=Summer 2011 |title='Remember Bunker Hill!'? |magazine=MHQ: The Quarterly Journal of Military History |pages=72–77 |volume=23 |issue=4 |id={{ProQuest|870391600}}}}</ref> The attendees included 190 Revolutionary War veterans,<ref name="MassMoments June 17, 1825">{{cite web |date=June 17, 1825 |title=Bostonians Lay Cornerstone for Bunker Hill Monument |url=https://www.massmoments.org/moment-details/bostonians-lay-cornerstone-for-bunker-hill-monument.html |access-date=March 8, 2026 |website=MassMoments}}</ref>{{Efn|These included 40 veterans of the battle itself,<ref name="Cameron ch. Transient Cornerstone" /><ref name="Warren p. 145" /> though one source cited the 190 soldiers as being Bunker Hill veterans.<ref name="MassMoments June 17, 1825" /> Another source described the procession as having 200 total veterans.<ref name="Packard p. 14" />}} hailing from across Massachusetts and the Eastern United States.<ref name="Warren p. 146">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|page=146}}</ref> They marched from the [[Massachusetts State House]] to Breed's Hill, where Daniel Webster addressed the audience;<ref name="Frothingham p. 344" /><ref name="BB pp. 15–16">{{harvnb|Brouillette|Brown|2000|ps=.|pages=15–16}}</ref><ref name="Packard p. 14">{{harvnb|Packard|1853|ps=.|page=14}}</ref> he sold the [[copyright]] to his speech for $600{{efn-ur|Equivalent to ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=600|start_year=1825|r=-3|fmt=c}} in {{Inflation/year|index=US-GDP}}{{Inflation/fn|index=US-GDP}}}} to help fund construction.<ref name="Cameron ch. Transient Cornerstone" /> Lafayette, who performed the ceremony, received a piece of the Warren monument from the Grand Lodge of Massachusetts.<ref name="p5094078492">{{cite news |last=Stone |first=Leon W. |date=June 2, 1956 |title=Shrine to Display Pageantry; Bunker Hill Salute Set: Monument Ceremony |work=The Christian Science Monitor |page=8 |issn=0882-7729 |id={{ProQuest|509407849}}}}</ref><ref name="p509407849" /> In recognition of the Grand Lodge's commemoration efforts,<ref name="Cameron ch. Transient Cornerstone" /> the BHMA invited the fraternity's Grand Master to conduct the services.<ref name="Frothingham p. 343" /><ref name="BB pp. 15–16" /> Rev. Joseph Thaxter, who had been a Continental Army chaplain during the battle,<ref name="Warren p. 146" /> officiated as chaplain.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=EhUpxD8ePjoC&q=%22leavitt+thaxter%22+mayhew&pg=PA85 |title=Thaxter prayer, Annals of the American Pulpit, William Buell Sprague, 1865 |access-date=April 9, 2018|last1=Sprague |first1=William Buell |year=1865 |postscript=none}}; {{cite book|url=https://archive.org/details/historytownhing01seymgoog |page=[https://archive.org/details/historytownhing01seymgoog/page/n319 281] |title=History of the Town of Hingham, Massachusetts, Thomas Tracy Bouve, 1893 |year=1893 |publisher=Pub. by the town |access-date=April 9, 2018}}</ref> The artifacts were later moved to a new cornerstone at the obelisk's northeastern corner.<ref name="Packard p. 16" /><ref name="Cameron ch. Transient Cornerstone" /><ref name="Warren p. 205">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|page=205}}</ref>

=== Obelisk construction<span class="anchor" id="Construction"></span> ===

==== Initial progress ====
A group of five men, including Baldwin, were appointed to a construction committee on July 12, 1825.<ref name="Warren p. 190">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|page=190}}</ref> By that September, the BHMA had raised $54,433.07{{efn-ur|Equivalent to ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=54433.07|start_year=1825|r=-3|fmt=c}} in {{Inflation/year|index=US-GDP}}{{Inflation/fn|index=US-GDP}}}}—more than half the $100,000 construction cost—and its directors voted to begin construction.<ref name="Warren p. 198">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|page=198}}</ref> Baldwin resigned not long afterward,<ref name="Warren p. 194" /><ref name="BB p. 15" /> and Willard was rehired that October to carry out the design.<ref name="n192875751" /><ref name="Frothingham p. 346">{{Harvnb|Frothingham|1851|page=346|ps=.}}</ref> Willard refused to be paid more than a small salary, and he also agreed to donate $1,000 for the obelisk's construction.{{efn-ur|Equivalent to ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=1000|start_year=1825|r=-3|fmt=c}} in {{Inflation/year|index=US-GDP}}{{Inflation/fn|index=US-GDP}}}}<ref name="Frothingham p. 346" /><ref name="Packard p. 17">{{harvnb|Packard|1853|ps=.|page=17}}</ref> James S. Savage was hired to construct the monument.<ref name="Frothingham p. 346" /><ref name="Packard p. 18">{{harvnb|Packard|1853|ps=.|page=18}}</ref> The BHMA had raised $64,010.55 before construction started.{{efn-ur|Equivalent to ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=64010.55|start_year=1825|r=-3|fmt=c}} in {{Inflation/year|index=US-GDP}}{{Inflation/fn|index=US-GDP}}}}<ref name="Frothingham p. 347" /> Only about half this amount could be used for the obelisk itself; the remainder was allocated toward land acquisition and other matters.<ref name="Frothingham p. 347">{{Harvnb|Frothingham|1851|page=347|ps=.}}</ref><ref name="Packard p. 21">{{harvnb|Packard|1853|ps=.|page=21}}</ref>

Before construction formally commenced, Willard visited various quarries to examine granite, reportedly walking {{Convert|300|mi}} in doing so.<ref name="Cameron ch. The Leading Character">{{harvnb|Cameron|1953|ps=.|loc=chapter "The Leading Character"}}</ref> In November 1825, workers began landscaping the site,<ref name="BB p. 16" /> and the BHMA decided to obtain granite from [[Quincy, Massachusetts]], a southern suburb of Boston.<ref name="Packard p. 18" /><ref name="Warren p. 203">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|page=203}}</ref> The BHMA paid [[Gridley Bryant]] $325 for the right to quarry the granite{{efn-ur|Equivalent to ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=325|start_year=1825|r=-3|fmt=c}} in {{Inflation/year|index=US-GDP}}{{Inflation/fn|index=US-GDP}}}}<ref name="Warren p. 203" /> from an outcropping that became known as the Bunker Hill Ledge.<ref name="Packard p. 18" /> By quarrying its own granite, the BHMA saved an estimated $60,000 in construction costs;{{efn-ur|Equivalent to ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=60000|start_year=1825|r=-3|fmt=c}} in {{Inflation/year|index=US-GDP}}{{Inflation/fn|index=US-GDP}}}}<ref name="Warren p. 204">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|page=204}}</ref> it could spend {{Convert|5.40|$/yd3}}, while a typical quarry would have charged {{Convert|24.30|$/yd3}}.<ref name="The Civil Engineer">The Civil Engineer's Pocket-Book, Trautwine et al., 19th Edition, 1909</ref> To save additional money, the BHMA used larger granite blocks, which required fewer stones.<ref name="BB p. 16" /> The granite blocks, averaging {{convert|2|cuyd|m3|spell=in|1}} each, were quarried by wedging<ref name="The Civil Engineer" /> and were then [[Stone-dressing|dressed]] and trimmed to fit the obelisk.<ref name="Warren p. 213">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|page=213}}</ref><ref name="Cameron ch. Beacon for Mariners">{{harvnb|Cameron|1953|ps=.|loc=chapter "Beacon for Mariners"}}</ref> The foundation was being constructed by 1826.<ref name="BB p. 16" /><ref name="SE pp. 55, 66">{{harvnb|Snell|Evans|1982|ps=.|pages=55, 66}}</ref>

The [[Granite Railway]]—a [[Broad-gauge railway|broad-gauge]], horse-drawn railway from Quincy—was constructed to transport the granite blocks.{{Efn|Although contemporary sources described the Granite Railway as the United States' first railway, several others had preceded it.<ref>{{cite journal | last=Gamst | first=Frederick C. | title=The Context and Significance of America's First Railroad, on Boston's Beacon Hill | journal=Technology and Culture | volume=33 | issue=1 |year=1992 | issn=1097-3729 | doi=10.1353/tech.1992.0148 | pages=66–67 | url=https://muse.jhu.edu/article/888338 | access-date=March 9, 2026| url-access=subscription }}</ref>}}<ref name="p873980270" /> Although the railway became operational on October 7, 1826,<ref name="p873980270">{{cite magazine |date=February 14, 1977 |title=Inside: Happy Birthday? |magazine=Railway Age |page=3 |volume=178 |issue=3 |id={{ProQuest|873980270}}}}</ref> a contract to transport the granite was not signed until early 1827.<ref name="Frothingham p. 346" /><ref name="Packard p. 18" /> The foundation was completed that July.<ref name="Packard p. 19">{{harvnb|Packard|1853|ps=.|page=19}}</ref> Since the Granite Railway ran only to the [[Neponset River]] waterfront in Quincy,<ref name="BB p. 16" /> the blocks had to be transferred to [[barge]]s, then transferred again in Charlestown.<ref name="Packard p. 18" /><ref name="Warren p. 209">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|page=209}}</ref> To carry the blocks up approximately {{Convert|84|ft}}, an [[inclined railway]] was built in Charlestown, ascending to the obelisk's southeastern corner. Within one year, the Granite Railway had transported {{Convert|3000|ST|LT t}} of granite.<ref name="Warren p. 209" /><ref name="commonwealth-7653c321v">{{cite news |date=June 15, 1929 |title=Monument Marks Site of Battle |url=https://www.digitalcommonwealth.org/book_viewer/commonwealth:7653c321v#?xywh=8434%2C636%2C4231%2C5296&cv=4 |access-date=March 9, 2026 |website=Charlestown News |page=5 |via=Digital Commonwealth}}</ref> The repeated transfers damaged the blocks and caused delays,<ref name="Frothingham p. 346" /><ref name="Packard p. 18" /> and in 1828 the BHMA decided to have oxen transport the granite directly to Breed's Hill.<ref name="Heitert p. 38">{{Harvnb|Heitert|2009|page=38|ps=.}}</ref><ref name="BB p. 17">{{harvnb|Brouillette|Brown|2000|ps=.|page=17}}</ref>

==== Monetary issues and delays ====
Twice during construction, the BHMA ran out of money to complete the obelisk; consequently, construction was suspended for a combined ten years.<ref name="p2629773602">{{Cite magazine |date=May 1, 1889 |title=The Completion of the Bunker Hill Monument Due to the Work of Women |magazine=Business Woman's Journal |pages=82–83 |volume=1 |issue=3 |id={{ProQuest|2629773602}}}}</ref> The first time was in 1828,<ref name="BB p. 17" /> when the BHMA obtained various loans in a futile attempt to keep the project solvent.<ref name="Frothingham p. 347" /><ref name="Warren p. 215">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|page=215}}</ref> The association reserved a {{Convert|600|by|400|ft|adj=on}} plot around the obelisk and placed a [[mortgage]] on the remaining, non-reserved land.<ref name="BB p. 17" /><ref name="Warren pp. 214–215">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|pages=214–215}}</ref> In January 1829, work was suspended after the loans were depleted.<ref name="Frothingham p. 347" /><ref name="Packard p. 19" /> Although $56,525.19 had been spent on construction to date,{{efn-ur|Equivalent to ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=56525.19|start_year=1829|r=-3|fmt=c}} in {{Inflation/year|index=US-GDP}}{{Inflation/fn|index=US-GDP}}}} most of these expenses had been allocated to the foundation.<ref name="Warren p. 215" /> Fourteen courses of granite had been laid, bringing the obelisk to a height of about {{Convert|38|ft}},<ref name="Cameron ch. Beacon for Mariners" /><ref name="Frothingham p. 347" /><ref name="Warren p. 215" /> and there was enough stone to build another {{Convert|18|ft}}.<ref name="Packard p. 19" /> A temporary roof was built atop the completed section of the obelisk.<ref name="Warren p. 253">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|page=253}}</ref> The BHMA unsuccessfully asked the General Court for permission to raise money through a [[lottery]].<ref name="Warren p. 234">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|page=234}}</ref><ref name="Packard pp. 22–23">{{harvnb|Packard|1853|ps=.|pages=22–23}}</ref> The group also solicited subscriptions from Boston residents<ref name="Warren p. 234" /> and appealed to women's groups in New England.<ref name="Packard pp. 22–23" /> The women's groups raised $2,000,{{efn-ur|Equivalent to ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=2000|start_year=1829|r=-3|fmt=c}} in {{Inflation/year|index=US-GDP}}{{Inflation/fn|index=US-GDP}}}}<ref name="Packard pp. 22–23" /> as each woman and child could contribute only $1 to that effort.{{efn-ur|Equivalent to ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=1|start_year=1829|r=0|fmt=c}} in {{Inflation/year|index=US-GDP}}{{Inflation/fn|index=US-GDP}}}}<ref name="Cameron ch. Beacon for Mariners" />

In 1831, the BHMA's officers passed stringent restrictions on the sale of land or material, but these were rescinded the next year.<ref name="Warren pp. 244–247">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|pages=244–247}}</ref> The association also made personal appeals to numerous wealthy Bostonians, to little avail,<ref name="Packard p. 27">{{harvnb|Packard|1853|ps=.|page=27}}</ref> while donations from the general public had slowed considerably.<ref name="Purcell p. 66">{{harvnb|Purcell|2003|ps=.|page=66}}</ref> In April 1833, the philanthropist [[Amos Lawrence]] appealed to the [[Massachusetts Charitable Mechanic Association]] for funds,<ref name="Frothingham p. 347" /><ref name="Packard p. 28">{{harvnb|Packard|1853|ps=.|page=28}}</ref> but nothing came of this proposal, despite sizable support for it.<ref name="Packard p. 28" /><ref name="Frothingham p. 348">{{Harvnb|Frothingham|1851|page=348|ps=.}}</ref> By that year, the association's debts totaled $30,000.{{efn-ur|Equivalent to ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=30000|start_year=1834|r=-3|fmt=c}} in {{Inflation/year|index=US-GDP}}{{Inflation/fn|index=US-GDP}}}}<ref name="Frothingham p. 348" /><ref name="Warren p. 279">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|page=279}}</ref> The association planned to sell the plots around the obelisk,<ref name="BB p. 17" /><ref name="SE pp. 160–161">{{harvnb|Snell|Evans|1982|ps=.|pages=160–161}}</ref> and it formed a committee, which suggested reducing the obelisk's height to save money.<ref name="Frothingham p. 348" /><ref>{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|pages=273–275}}</ref> That June, the BHMA agreed to sell off the non-reserved land,<ref name="Frothingham p. 348" /><ref name="Warren pp. 276–277">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|pages=276–277}}</ref> anticipating that it could raise $25,000{{efn-ur|Equivalent to ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=25000|start_year=1834|r=-3|fmt=c}} in {{Inflation/year|index=US-GDP}}{{Inflation/fn|index=US-GDP}}}} by selling off {{Convert|127000|ft2}}.<ref name="Warren pp. 276–277" /><ref name="BB p. 18">{{harvnb|Brouillette|Brown|2000|ps=.|page=18}}</ref> The land was thus split into 115 lots.<ref name="Heitert p. 38" /><ref name="SE p. 68">{{harvnb|Snell|Evans|1982|ps=.|page=68}}</ref> The association also agreed to shorten the obelisk to {{Convert|159.5|ft}}, and its officers rehired Willard in June 1834 to oversee the design.<ref name="Frothingham pp. 348–349">{{Harvnb|Frothingham|1851|pages=348–349|ps=.}}</ref><ref name="Warren p. 278">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|page=278}}</ref>

Work continued until November 1835, when funding shortages stopped construction again.<ref name="Warren p. 278" /> During this time, $20,000–23,000 was raised for the project; the Ladies' Fund raised about $3,000, while the remainder was raised by the Mechanic Association.<ref name="Packard p. 30">{{harvnb|Packard|1853|ps=.|page=30}}</ref><ref name="Frothingham p. 349">{{Harvnb|Frothingham|1851|page=349|ps=.}}</ref><ref name="Warren p. 278" /> Nearly the entire amount was spent on the newly-built portion of the obelisk,<ref name="Packard p. 30" /><ref name="Frothingham p. 349" /> which had reached {{Convert|80|ft}} when funds were depleted.<ref name="Packard p. 30" /><ref name="n192820877">{{Cite news |last=Courier |first=Ann-Mary |date=August 20, 1972 |title=MDC may surrender Bunker Hill to US |via=Newspapers.com |url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-boston-globe-mdc-may-surrender-bunke/192820877/ |access-date=March 7, 2026 |work=The Boston Globe |issn=0743-1791 |pages=32}}</ref>{{Efn|A figure of {{Convert|82|ft}} is also given.<ref name="Frothingham p. 349" /> Another source cites a figure of {{Convert|85|ft}}, with 32 courses having been built.<ref name="Cameron ch. Beacon for Mariners" />}} In 1838, the BHMA further reduced the north–south length of its reserved area from {{Convert|600|to|417|ft}},<ref name="Warren p. 280">{{harvnb|Warren|1877|ps=.|p=280}}</ref> bringing the site to about {{Convert|4|acre}}.<ref name="BB p. 18" /> After the remaining two-thirds of the original site was sold in September 1839, the BHMA not only paid off its entire debt but also recorded a $1,767.57 surplus.{{efn-ur|Equivalent to ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=1767.57|start_year=1839|r=-3|fmt=c}} in {{Inflation/year|index=US-GDP}}{{Inflation/fn|index=US-GDP}}}}<ref name="BB p. 18" /> Excess soil from Monument Square's western and eastern slopes was relocated to the square's northern boundary, filling in a depression there.<ref name="BB p. 18" /><ref name="Heitert p. 38" /><ref name="SE p. 77">{{harvnb|Snell|Evans|1982|ps=.|page=77}}</ref>

==== Completion ====
[[File:1840 Bunker Hill Monument Boston detail from Freemens Quick Step.png|thumb|Construction progress as of 1840|alt=Sketch of the Bunker Hill Monument being built. An American flag is hoisted above the portion of the monument that had been constructed.]]
In 1839, Lawrence and [[Judah Touro]] each pledged $10,000{{efn-ur|name=10000-amount-1825}} to match local contributions to spur the monument's completion.<ref name="Frothingham p. 349" /><ref name="Packard p. 31">{{harvnb|Packard|1853|ps=.|page=31}}</ref> The terms of the men's gifts required private donors to raise an additional $25,000<ref>{{cite web |last=Tenorio |first=Rich |date=June 5, 2025 |work=Jewish Journal |title=
Remembering the Jewish philanthropist who helped build the Bunker Hill Monument

== Management and operational history<span class="anchor" id="Management"></span> ==

=== BHMA operation ===
[[File:Bunker Hill Monument 20201128 05.jpg|thumb|View of the obelisk from a distance, seen along Monument Street|alt=A street with buildings on either side as seen from an intersection. In the background is an obelisk.]]
When the monument opened, the BHMA charged admission for the climb,<ref name="BB p. 30" /><ref name="Heitert p. 39">{{Harvnb|Heitert|2009|page=39|ps=.}}</ref> but many visitors hesitated to pay the fee.<ref name="p503757806" /> The association also raised money through donations.<ref name="Heitert p. 39" /> The monument had a [[guestbook]], whose signatories included King [[Edward VII]] of Britain and the future British prime minister [[Winston Churchill]].<ref name="p815738550">{{Cite news |date=June 16, 1940 |title=Winston Churchill Made Bee Line for Bunker Hill Monument: England's Present Prime Minister Climbed the 294 Steps, Then Studied Pictures of Revolutionary Heroes When He Visited Famous Shaft |work=The Boston Daily Globe |issn=0743-1791 |page=B42  |id={{ProQuest|815738550}}}}</ref> The monument hosted Bunker Hill Day commemorations on June 17 each year,<ref name="Cameron ch. Today">{{harvnb|Cameron|1953|ps=.|loc=chapter "Today"}}</ref><ref name="Wilson pp. 34–35">{{harvnb|Wilson|2004|ps=.|pages=34–35}}</ref> a day known as the "glorious seventeenth".<ref name="NYT g889">{{cite news |date=June 18, 1932 |title=Observes Bunker Hill Day; Boston Celebrates Anniversary of Revolutionary Battle. |url=https://www.nytimes.com/1932/06/18/archives/observes-bunker-hill-day-boston-celebrates-anniversary-of.html |access-date=March 13, 2026 |work=The New York Times |issn=0362-4331 |language=en-US |agency=Associated Press}}</ref> Bunker Hill Day, originally a solemn commemoration, evolved over time into a festive event.<ref name="Cameron ch. Today" /> American flags were displayed in the windows during major holidays and other special occasions approved by the BHMA.<ref name="n193032754">{{Cite news |date=April 15, 1917 |title=Orders Lacking for Flags on Bunker Hill Monument |via=Newspapers.com |url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-boston-globe-orders-lacking-for-flag/193032754/ |access-date=March 9, 2026 |work=The Boston Daily Globe |issn=0743-1791 |pages=10}}</ref> Over the next century, the association's members included 6 [[Harvard University]] presidents, 12 U.S. presidents, 20 Boston mayors, and a myriad of Massachusetts governors and military personnel.<ref name="Cameron ch. Today" />

==== 1840s to 1870s ====
George M. Dexter began devising plans for a permanent entrance building in 1843, but he died while drawing the plans.<ref name="HV p. 16">{{harvnb|Hardy-Heck-Moore Inc.|Volz & Associates Inc.|2011|ps=.|page=16}}</ref><ref name="BB p. 30">{{harvnb|Brouillette|Brown|2000|ps=.|page=30}}</ref> William S. Park completed the plans, which were then delayed for lack of funding.<ref name="BB p. 30" /><ref name="SE p. 92">{{harvnb|Snell|Evans|1982|ps=.|page=92}}</ref> Landscaping continued for years after the obelisk's official dedication. The new landscaping included a brick sidewalk, fence, and trees at Monument Square's perimeter, along with eight entrances and two concentric walkways in the park.<ref name="BB p. 20" /><ref name="Heitert p. 39" /> A replica of the site's original Warren monument was placed inside the obelisk in 1845.<ref name="p509407849" /><ref name="n193021748" /> The BHMA voted in 1849 to exclude inscriptions of names and dates from the obelisk;<ref name="BB p. 23">{{harvnb|Brouillette|Brown|2000|ps=.|page=23}}</ref><ref name="n193032104">{{Cite news |last=H.H.E. |date=June 29, 1914 |title=The Bunker Hill Inscription |via=Newspapers.com |url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/boston-evening-transcript-the-bunker-hil/193032104/ |access-date=March 9, 2026 |work=Boston Evening Transcript |pages=8}}</ref> these inscriptions had been part of early plans, but the BHMA felt the inscriptions would detract from the obelisk's symbolism.<ref name="Purcell p. 64">{{harvnb|Purcell|2003|ps=.|page=64}}</ref>

[[Henry Dexter (sculptor)|Henry Dexter]] was commissioned to design a sculpture of Joseph Warren in 1850.<ref name="BB p. 23" /> A wooden entrance building, the first Bunker Hill Lodge, was completed in 1857<ref name="BB p. 23" /><ref name="HV p. 16" /> and was the first significant modification of the monument.<ref name="Heitert p. 39" /> The first Bunker Hill Lodge, a single-story edifice, was intended as a temporary structure but lasted for over four decades.<ref name="BB p. 23" /><ref name="HV p. 16" /> The Joseph Warren sculpture was displayed in this wooden building.<ref name="HV p. 15" /><ref name="BB p. 23" /> During the [[American Civil War]], the monument remained open, and over 40,000 people signed its guestbooks.<ref name="MacQuarrie b941">{{cite web |last=MacQuarrie |first=Brian |date=April 13, 2021 |title=In a surprise, Bunker Hill guestbooks from Civil War emerge in auction |url=https://www.bostonglobe.com/2021/04/13/metro/surprise-bunker-hill-guestbooks-civil-war-emerge-auction/ |access-date=March 15, 2026 |website=The Boston Globe |issn=0743-1791}}</ref> A wooden flagpole mast was mounted from the top of the obelisk in 1861.<ref name="BB p. 23" /><ref name="SE p. 90" /> To avoid detracting from the obelisk's appearance, this flagpole was removed in 1866,<ref name="BB p. 23" /><ref name="SE p. 90">{{harvnb|Snell|Evans|1982|ps=.|page=90}}</ref> replaced the next year by a {{Convert|140|ft|4=-tall|adj=mid}} freestanding flagpole northeast of the obelisk.<ref name="BB p. 23" /><ref name="SE p. 91">{{harvnb|Snell|Evans|1982|ps=.|page=91}}</ref>

During the early 1870s, the BHMA undertook landscape upgrades to the site.<ref name="BB pp. 26–28">{{harvnb|Brouillette|Brown|2000|ps=.|pages=26–28}}</ref><ref name="SE pp. 96–98">{{harvnb|Snell|Evans|1982|ps=.|pages=96–98}}</ref> The association removed the freestanding flagpole in 1874,<ref name="BB p. 23" /><ref name="Heitert p. 39" /> after which the two poles atop the obelisk displayed flags only during special occasions.<ref name="BB p. 23" /><ref name="SE p. 91" /> For the battle's centennial, a massive procession marched from [[Downtown Boston]] to the monument.<ref name="p136661568">{{Cite magazine |date=June 24, 1875 |title=The Bunker Hill Celebration. |magazine=New York Observer and Chronicle |page=198 |volume=53 |issue=25 |id={{ProQuest|136661568}}}}</ref> The BHMA subsequently commissioned granite markers in 1876, commemorating important events in the battle,<ref name="BB p. 26" /> and it installed a fence around the purported site of Warren's death that year.<ref name="Heitert p. 39" /> The BHMA established a fund in the 1870s to replace the wooden structure with a permanent building<ref name="BB p. 30" /> and another fund for a proper memorial to Colonel Prescott.<ref name="n193106532" />

==== 1970s to 1990s ====
When the NPS took over, the site had again deteriorated, and the obelisk had broken granite and failing masonry joints. Modifications over the years were inconsistent with the original design.<ref name="BB p. 63" /> The Freedom Trail was extended to the monument  in 1977.<ref name="BB p. 63" /><ref name="Heitert p. 44" /> The monument was closed for repairs in December 1979, reopening the following November.<ref name="n192813836" /> The repairs included making the obelisk's foundation waterproof, repointing the stones, and adding fences.<ref name="n192813836">{{Cite news |date=November 11, 1980 |title=New England News in Brief |via=Newspapers.com |url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-boston-globe-new-england-news-in-bri/192813836/ |access-date=March 6, 2026 |work=The Boston Globe |issn=0743-1791 |pages=16}}</ref><ref name="BB p. 67">{{harvnb|Brouillette|Brown|2000|ps=.|page=67}}</ref> In addition, drainage and lighting systems were upgraded.<ref name="BB p. 67" /> Thomas Mahlstedt also began conducting the first archeological study of the site in 1980, during the renovation.<ref name="Heitert p. 53">{{Harvnb|Heitert|2009|page=53|ps=.}}</ref> His study, split up into several phases, uncovered numerous artifacts, of which only one object (a spoon) was deemed to have intrinsic value.<ref name="Heitert pp. 60–61">{{Harvnb|Heitert|2009|pages=60–61|ps=.}}</ref>

==Impact and legacy==

=== Reception and influence ===
Until the [[Washington Monument]] was completed in 1885,<ref name="p31828907764">{{Cite news |last=Abel |first=David |date=June 11, 2006 |title=Getting to the Point: In Your Face, Short on Resources --the Bunker Hill Monument, Like the 1775 Battle It Recalls, Has a History of Hanging Tough. Zakim Bunker Hill Memorial Bridge|Zakim Bunker Hill Bridge]]'s towers.<ref name="p405241505">{{Cite news |last=Howe |first=Peter J. |date=August 10, 1998 |title=Masterpiece or Monster |work=The Boston Globe |issn=0743-1791 |page=C3  |id={{ProQuest|405241505}}}}</ref><ref name="p198170263">{{Cite magazine |last=Nasvik |first=Joe |date=Sep 2003 |title=The Zakim, Bunker Hill Bridge |magazine=Concrete Construction |pages=32–34, 36 |volume=48 |issue=9 |id={{ProQuest|198170263}}}}</ref> After the Bunker Hill Monument was completed, granite was more widely used in buildings across the U.S.<ref name="Heitert p. 38" />

=== Landmark designations ===
The Bunker Hill Monument became a [[National Historic Landmark]] (NHL) on January 20, 1961.<ref>{{cite web | title=List of NHLs by State | website=National Historic Landmarks (U.S. National Park Service) | date=May 4, 1970 | url=https://www.nps.gov/subjects/nationalhistoriclandmarks/list-of-nhls-by-state.htm | access-date=February 14, 2026}}</ref> The NHL designation was granted in part for the obelisk's role as a major early war memorial<ref name="nhl">{{cite web|url={{NHLS url|id=66000138}}|title=NHL nomination for Bunker Hill Monument|publisher=National Park Service|access-date=February 19, 2015}}</ref> and in part because the site itself had historical significance.<ref name="HV p. 8">{{harvnb|Hardy-Heck-Moore Inc.|Volz & Associates Inc.|2011|ps=.|page=8}}</ref> It was listed on the [[National Register of Historic Places]] (NRHP) in 1966, when the [[National Historic Preservation Act of 1966]] went into effect.<ref>{{cite book |author=United States. National Park Service |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=u3p1AAAAMAAJ&pg=PR9 |title=The National Register of Historic Places |author2=Preservation Press |publisher=National Park Service. |year=1991 |isbn=978-0-942063-21-9 |pages=9, 360}}</ref> The NRHP nomination form cited the site's military significance.<ref name="HV p. 8" /> Also in 1966, the state government enacted further protections, prohibiting the use of [[eminent domain]] to acquire the land.<ref name="The Standard-Times 1966" />

The monument is part of two [[Historic districts in the United States|United States historic districts]]. It is one of eight sites in the {{Convert|43|acre|adj=on}} [[Boston National Historical Park]],<ref name="TCLF x237">{{cite web |date=September 12, 2020 |title=Boston National Historical Park |url=https://www.tclf.org/boston-national-historical-park |access-date=January 24, 2026 |website=TCLF}}</ref> which was designated in 1974.<ref name="p822110000">{{Cite news |last=Wermiel |first=Stephen |date=July 11, 1974 |title=House panel approves bill on US historical park in Boston |work=The Boston Daily Globe |issn=0743-1791 |pages=22  |id={{ProQuest|822110000}}}}</ref><ref name="n191931631">{{Cite news |last=Carr |first=Stanley |date=November 17, 1974 |title=Boston's historic sites get federal transfusion |via=Newspapers.com |url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-press-democrat-bostons-historic-sit/191931631/ |access-date=February 23, 2026 |work=The Press Democrat |pages=60 }}</ref> The monument and park are also part of the [[Monument Square Historic District (Charlestown, Boston, Massachusetts)|Monument Square Historic District]], designated in 1987<ref name="hd_nr">{{cite web|url=https://mhc-macris.net/details?mhcid=bos.cm|title=NRHP nomination for Monument Square Historic District|publisher=Commonwealth of Massachusetts|access-date=February 19, 2015}}</ref> partly for its architectural character.<ref name="BB p. 89" /> Outside Monument Square, the remainder of the former battlefield is not covered by any historic district, prompting local residents to create the Charlestown Historic Battlefield District Committee in 2023.<ref name="WBUR m436">{{cite web |last=Shenoy |first=Rupa |date=June 20, 2023 |title=Some Charlestown residents say Battle of Bunker Hill isn't properly recognized |url=https://www.wbur.org/news/2023/06/20/battle-bunker-hill-hisotric-battlefield-designation-charlestown |access-date=March 15, 2026 |website=WBUR News}}</ref>
